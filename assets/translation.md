**29. December 1939**

**Taslicay (Asia Minor)**

**My beloved parents!**

I, old butter-fingers, have of course forgotten once again to congratulate you on your 28th wedding anniversary, which I could have done so nicely with the telegram from Bazirgan on the 23rd. Would that I had! Now I begin my travel report thinking of you both with heartfelt thoughts, with the earnest wish that we may celebrate today's day in the coming year together with all our loved ones, and that you both may spend a carefree, pleasant time in best health in Tehran until your hopefully soon departure!

Our farewell today 6 days ago came so suddenly, and everything I had still wanted to tell you remained stuck in my throat. With all the overcome and upcoming difficulties, we had no time to think of the farewell, and that was good so. It was also good that everything went so fast; for when the car picked us up and the separation thereby only became a certainty, it hit us especially hard. It was a blessing that I became particularly strongly aware in the last days before the departure of the feeling of so many truly valuable friendships shown to me, for if anything helped to make the farewell so hard for me, I know that you, beloved parents, are not alone! While we had to wait almost 2 ½ hours for the departure of the vehicle in the garage, where I hoped you might come by once more, I thought about the last 2 years with the result of deep gratitude for having gathered many vital experiences, found loyal friends, and not least, being able to escape the immediate danger zone.

Around 4:30 / 5:00, the fourth fellow traveler finally arrived: an Armenian girl, with an enormous amount of luggage and provision baskets that were more than sufficient for our trip, though meant for others; furthermore, a host of sisters, brothers, and female friends. The third fellow traveler was a young man who somehow belonged to this girl and had waited with full impatience. Now the luggage had to be stowed, which did not happen without noisy differences of opinion and for which our things were first thrown out again. Finally, at 5 o'clock we drove off; the chauffeur, we three men, I in the middle, in the back. Vehicle and driver were excellent. We left Tehran in the twilight; I was barely occupied with my backward-looking thoughts. Spoken was hardly a word.

Even before Qazvin we ran into a light snowfall, which caused mild concern in us. The plain was white, even if covered with only a very delicate layer of snow. At 8:30 we stopped in front of the "Grand Hotel" in Qazvin for a short rest. Strauss had spent 4 days there on his journey to Tehran and rushed in remarkable joy of reunion to the venerable radio set, without noteworthy success. Meanwhile, I ordered bread and tea. Our bread was meant to spare that of the affluent Landrock, which we had taken along; he took 8 Toman from us for the meager snack. Then we went on through the snowfall toward Zanjan, where we arrived around midnight. We were very tired and requested a room in the only hotel of the place. Most rooms were locked and the key could not be found. After the girl and the young man were accommodated, they let us in by force into a room as well, though with only one bed. As it was bitterly cold, two rooms seemed unsuitable, and we lay down together. I was already asleep when the young man...

Hamid-Aga, the garage owner to whom Mr. Fischer had recommended us, explained to us over tea that on Thursday a truck would leave for Trebizond [Trabzon] that wanted to take us. He would try to accommodate us there. We then moved into the Grand Hotel as quarters and got the allegedly last room, which fully satisfied our expectations. We ate and decided to go to the cinema; there we slept quite well and then continued sleeping in the hotel later. The next morning the luggage was there and I changed my shoes in the garage first, as they had pinched me severely. Then came a discussion with Hamid-Aga. Communication was miserable, so he brought us to a pharmacy opposite, where a sympathetic older gentleman translated via French. Strauss mentioned in doing so that we were Jewish emigrants, whereupon he proved to be a Lithuanian Jew and was helpful to us in a touching way. He ran with us to his friend, a transport specialist, and then sent us with his clerk into the bazaar, where we made the trip to Trebizond perfect with the manager of a firm for 130 Toman.

We then ate a portion of roast chicken in the hotel, which filled both of us up despite a huge appetite. Then we went into the bazaar and immediately found Mr. Fischer's acquaintance, a man sitting with crossed legs, a black-framed moon face, asked for his English-speaking son, who was brought to us right away and negotiated the bus for 1 Toman for us. About 10 km before Maku another stop was made, and there stood our Chevrolet again. We were greeted by the two drivers with noise and dragged into a dive bar where we ate dinner: hand-towels of lesser quality, an excellent dish and a porridge made of flour, sugar, and mutton fat. We ripped a rag off the hand-towel, packed a piece of cheese, fat, or porridge on top with our fingers, rolled it up like a giant cigarette, and ate the whole thing; and because we were hungry it tasted excellent. At 11 o'clock we arrived in Maku.

**Karagossa, 28.12.39.**

From Tabriz to Maku, one could only describe the paths in short sections as roads. Mostly they lay below the level of a German dirt road. Nevertheless, the ride through the high mountain landscape was overall delightful, both by day and by night under clear visibility in the sharp moonlight against the mountains. In Maku there was no hotel, not even a teahouse. Our companions had beds for themselves, which they set up in the bus waiting room. For us, 2 benches were placed next to each other, a mattress laid on top, and so we had a very comfortable bed on which we slept well.

The next morning we saw the village, which sits in a narrow valley under a mighty, vertical rock wall on a scree slope made mostly of red-tinted houses. We found ourselves at the bottom of the valley; above us clung the houses up to 100m height like birds' nests, and then came the wall, which threatened to crush everything below it.

There was not much to attract us; for we had taken off our jackets to sleep, water for washing was non-existent, and brushing teeth was not even to be thought of. We stuffed our bellies full with tea, bread, and cheese again and drove on at 9 o'clock, now for the first time in the Chevrolet. As there were only 3 seats, the chauffeur had to sit on the mudguard and the manager drove a short distance together. Luckily it was only 4 km to Bazirgan, and the road was up above. So we arrived in good weather at the two massive buildings at the outermost northwest tip of Iran, in which the police and customs are housed. Then there were a few sheds for goods and a few...

Only then did we move a few hundred meters further, although the incline was small. Always and again it had to be shoveled. Sometimes it looked hopeless; and then we had to get out to help push. Already upon stepping out of the car one sank up to above the knee in the snow. The dreadfully cold air sliced the skin. Around the ears I had laid my woolen scarf. **Türkali, 3. January 1940.**

It falls hard for me to overcome myself to write. How immensely depressing this decade starts for us. We sit here in a dark hole, a few stacked stones stuck together with mud; the only inventory are an iron stove, a kerosene lamp, and 2 woven mats. The twilight is produced by a window in the ceiling, 20 cm square, which is drifted over with snow. And the whole thing is located in high mountains in an unimaginable mountain village. We had taken a desperate attempt to overcome the mountains and stuck short before the pass in meter-high snow in a horrific snowstorm. After hours of exertion we left sleds and luggage up there and saved ourselves and the horses back into this dump. But I will tell in order how it came to this!

The awakening in that lonely house took place on Dec 25th of last year. It was a wonderful sunny winter day, and as we stepped out in front of the house into the warm sun, before us the light-flooded snow landscape, we were reconciled with our fate. Then we also saw a friendly village in the valley a short distance away. We stomped without coats in the warm sun through the high snow; soon however my nose froze up. Therefore I went into the first house to warm up. We were received very friendly and recognized by the lists lying around that we were at the village elder's. Communication was impossible. Nevertheless we conversed for a while remarkably with our hands, while we were served tea. Then we walked further past a small facility with the war memorial and the school into an inn. There sat already our chauffeurs and we learned now that the village was called **Taslicay**, that in the night 35 degrees of cold had been, and we would now have to wait until gasoline from the 35km distant Karagossa arrived. The whole village ran together to marvel at us, including one who spoke some Persian. He undertook the communication subsequently. We then went back once more to fetch our coats, the writing pad, and playing cards, and then stayed into the night in the inn. First we learned the Turkish numbers; then I laid out a list of the most important words that we constantly needed, and finally I began this report. You, dear Papa, will probably search in vain with your finger on the map. When you go up from Baghdad with the Euphrates, and where it splits, follow the southern source stream, then you finally come to Karagossa (Karagöse) and right at the beginning, where the Euphrates is as wide as the Klia [river], lies Taslicay.

Most of the time of the day we spent playing cards. In between we joined in with the lunch that they left for us, as inedible. How good was the Iranian rice against this surrogate they called rice. Good rice is very expensive in Turkey, and now we understood why our chauffeur had smiled satisfied at the border for 25 pounds of rice 5 Lira duty...

The Chevrolet, which was 300m ahead of us, was already gone. The visibility in the hazy weather was miserable. Barely could one recognize the drifted ruts. So it happened that we got onto the spot where the Chevrolet had stood, in whose tracks stuck. During the monstrous exertions of the engine to get free, an axle broke, and with that it was completely over. We stomped disgruntled back into the village and rented through the mediation of the Persian-speaking person a sled. An hour later we loaded our luggage from the Ford onto the sled, except for the two large pieces of Strauss's, which were on the Chevrolet. Then we set off. I had my woolen scarf wrapped picturesquely around head and ears. Strauss had his ski cap, which was invaluable, and 2 left gloves; the right one he had lost on the way. Otherwise it was unthinkable uncomfortable. My feet froze in the mountain boots soon so that I could no longer move them. After a few kilometers the fat chauffeur of the Chevrolet came toward us; we stopped and he spoke excitedly with the driver. He gestured wild with both hands. As we drove further, we saw the mess. The car was a track off the road and overturned. The many boxes with goods lay peacefully with Strauss's suitcases scattered in the area. We loaded the two suitcases onto the sled and went further. At darkness we arrived in Karagossa, a larger village with electric lighting, according to the map the capital of the province Agri. In the hotel, Strauss pulled the shoes off my feet, which were completely frozen stiff. Under great pain I thawed out, while Strauss inspected our room and returned satisfied. Then we heard that the connection to Erzurum was impossible and went into the nearest kitchen to dine lavishly. As we returned to the hotel, we found barely a place. Only a few of those present consumed something. Most played "Puff" [backgammon], apparently the local national game; for anyone can do it and even children play it with ape-like speed. We soon went to our room, where it was freezing cold. Even when we lit the wooden logs in the stove, it became only temporarily insignificantly warmer. The beds were clean, and therefore I pulled off my clothes for the first time in a long time; but I could not get warm and so spent the night trying to get dressed again. I put on stockings and all the sweaters I had, put Strauss's ski cap on and tied my scarf over it; then I laid our two winter coats over me. When I finally fell asleep toward morning, it woke me up soon again facing the water pitcher, in which the water was frozen down to the bottom. Our morning toilet shaped up according to the cold and half-frozen we reached the guest room. Our 130 Toman were gone, we sat instead of in Trebizond in Karagossa and had to rely on ourselves alone. An unpleasant situation. Our day was filled with inquiries at the bus office when the road would be opened, targeting all chauffeurs, card and backgammon playing, eating, and in between I wrote on my report. The innkeepers of the hotel were charming. They were the only ones with whom we could communicate, as they spoke Persian and helped us in a touching way to get further. But the road was and remained closed for cars for an indefinite time, and there was no other way out than to decide for us on the 185km long ride on sleds.

At the first dawn of morning it went on in finely spraying rain. There were 3 sleds. 2 were added the evening before with a young Turk, his wife, 4 children, and luggage. Now it went into the feared mountains, constantly uphill, and we moved forward only at a walk. To our luck the rain stopped soon. Around 10 o'clock we had covered 22 km and stopped before the gendarmerie in Türkali, before us the high mountains, over which we had to go. The driver telephoned to Tahir, the day's goal, which is 18 km from here, and got word that the road was free. So we drove on. After a few hundred meters every trace lost itself in the high snow and we had to get out, because the horses could not pull the heavy sleds through. One went ahead to search for the path. If he sank only up to his knees in the snow, then we were on the right path. A heavy storm whipped us sharp ice crystals into the face, while we stomped uphill. Through the exertion we sweated despite the gruesome cold on the whole body. The higher we came, the sharper the storm became, the snow ever deeper. So we fought our way step-by-step forward for hours. As we were just before the ridge at a bend of the path, while I stomped ahead, I suddenly lay in the snow. The dreadful storm had blown me over. The horses stuck up to above the knee in snow. It was the end. We could not see 5m far. Beneath a gruesome howling the storm whipped the snow toward us. Added to this came the crying of the children from the covered sled. The driver and we both tried from top to bottom to make a path and vanished in the snowstorm, while we tried to get the horses free. Meanwhile the driver returned. Strauss remained vanished.
