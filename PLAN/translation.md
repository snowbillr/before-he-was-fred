**25. December 1939**

**Taslicay (Asia Minor)**

**My beloved parents!**

I, old butter-fingers, have of course forgotten once again to congratulate you on your 28th wedding anniversary, which I could have done so nicely with the telegram from Bazirgan on the 23rd of this month. Now I begin my travel report thinking of you both with heartfelt thoughts, with the earnest wish that we may celebrate today's day next year together with all our loved ones, and that you both may spend a carefree, pleasant time in best health in Tehran until your hopefully soon crossing!

Our farewell today 6 days ago came so suddenly, and everything I had still wanted to tell you remained stuck in my chest. With all the overcome and upcoming difficulties, we had had no time to think of the farewell, and that was good so. It was also good that everything then went so fast; for when the car picked us up and the separation thereby became a certainty, it hit us especially hard. It was a blessing that I could leave with the feeling of so many truly valuable friendships shown to me, of which I became particularly strongly aware in the last days before the departure. Even though this contributed a great deal to making the farewell so hard for me, I know for certain that you, beloved parents, are not alone! During the almost 2 hours that we still had to wait in the garage for the departure of the vehicle, where I hoped you might perhaps come by once more, I thought about the last 2 years, with the result of grateful contentment that I have gathered many vital experiences, found loyal friends, and not least was able to get you out of the immediate danger zone.

At a quarter to five the fourth fellow traveler finally arrived, an Armenian girl, with an enormous amount of luggage and provision baskets that would have been sufficient for our whole trip, and furthermore a host of sisters, brothers, and female friends. The third fellow traveler was a young man who somehow belonged to this girl and had waited full of impatience. Now the luggage had to be stowed, which did not happen without noisy differences of opinion and for which our things were first thrown out again. Finally, at 5 o'clock we drove off; the girl next to the chauffeur, we three men in the back, I in the middle. Vehicle and driver were excellent. We left Tehran in the twilight, I occupied with my retrospective thoughts. Hardly a word was spoken.

Even before Qazvin we ran into a light snowfall, which caused us mild concern. The desert was white, even if covered with only a very delicate layer of snow. At 7:30 we stopped in front of the "Grand Hotel" in Qazvin for a short rest. Strauss had spent 4 days there on his journey to Tehran and, in a surge of joy at the reunion, rushed to the venerable radio set, without noteworthy success. Meanwhile, I ordered borscht and tea. We still wanted to save our sandwiches. On account of the affluent impression we made, they took 8 Kran from us for this meager snack. Then we went on through the snowfall to Zanjan, where we arrived around midnight. We were very tired and requested a room in the only hotel of the place. Most rooms were locked and the key could not be found. After the girl and the boy were accommodated, a room could then be forced open for us as well, though with only one bed. As it was bitterly cold, that seemed quite favorable to us, and we lay down. I was already lying down when the boy...

Once again I had reason to thank fate that I sat in the back. At 10 o'clock we made a tea break in a little village named Taserkand, i.e. we both drank delicious warm milk. Lunch was in Mianeh (typed "Mayimäh"?), after we had crossed a high, heavily snowed mountain range, unlike many other cars that had gotten stuck in the snow. We ate good, cheap roast chicken with fried potatoes and then drove on, until Strauss and I suddenly both jumped up at the same time. For there, in front of a standalone teahouse, exactly 100 km before Tabriz, stood the bus with our luggage, which had already been on the road for 4 days. After Strauss had urged me at every difficult spot on the road to check whether I could still gather up a few of my postage stamps, this was finally a certain reassurance for me. Later it went in endless serpentines over a high mountain wrapped in clouds, where the chauffeur showed us the spot where, after the Crown Prince's wedding, 2 Turkish airplanes flew into the mountain and crashed, and at 6:30 we were in Tabriz.

Hamid-Aga, the garage owner to whom Mr. Fischer had recommended us, explained to us over tea that on Thursday a truck was to leave for Trebizond [Trabzon]. He would try to get us a place on it. We then took quarters in the Grand Hotel and got the allegedly last room, which fully satisfied our expectations, cleaned ourselves up, and decided to go to the cinema. There we then slept quite well, and continued doing so later in the hotel. The next morning the luggage was there, and in the garage I first changed my shoes, which had been giving me a hard time. Then another discussion with Hamid-Aga. Communication was miserable, so he brought us to a pharmacy opposite, where a sympathetic older gentleman interpreted via French. Strauss mentioned in doing so that we were Jewish emigrants, whereupon he introduced himself as a Lithuanian Jew and helped us in a touching way. He walked with us to his friend, a transport expert, and then sent us with his assistant into the bazaar, where we settled the trip to Trebizond for 130 Toman with the manager of a firm.

It was a 1.5-ton Chevrolet with room for 3 persons. Our luggage was loaded right away; we were to go as far as Bazirgan by bus, since the manager was riding in the truck until there. We then ate an excellent portion of partridge in the hotel, which filled both of us up despite our huge appetite. Then we went into the bazaar and immediately found Mr. Fischer's acquaintance, a black-framed moon face squatting cross-legged, asked for his English-speaking son, who was fetched for us at once, and acquired "bx" for "tl." (abbreviations unclear; probably a currency exchange). Then we said goodbye to Isaac Rabinowitsch, the pharmacist, and went to the bus garage. People were even squatting packed tightly in the aisle. We drove into the night, at first along the railway line to Julfa, which we crossed many times. Our goal for this day was Khoi. The next morning we strolled through the bazaar, where we each bought a pair of thick, colorful wool socks for 3 Kran, which actually look more like slippers and are worn by almost all people there. For lunch we drank excellent dugh [sour milk drink] and ate good kebab with it, and at 3 o'clock we drove on with an ancient bus that was mainly loaded with junk. We were only 7 people, and it went over high mountains. Slower than a pedestrian, the old box wheezed laboriously up in first gear, and every 2 km it was stopped to refill large amounts of carried water into the boiling radiator. Downhill it went fast, and finally came a broad river, along which we drove upstream for a stretch, to then stop in a small village at dusk. We drank tea in a picturesque opium den, scraped together a few more of your pistachios, Junilein, from the bottom of the briefcase, and then drove further into the dark mountains. About 10 km before Maku another stop was made, and there stood our Chevrolet again too. We were greeted noisily by the two and dragged into a dive where we had supper: "hand towels" of lesser quality, excellent mast [yogurt], and a porridge made of flour, sugar, and mutton fat. We tore a rag off the hand towel, packed a piece of cheese, mast, or porridge on it with our fingers, and rolled it up like a giant cigarette. Then we wolfed down the whole thing, and because we were hungry it tasted excellent. At 11 o'clock we arrived in Maku.

**Karagossa, 28.12.39.**

From Tabriz on, one could call the paths roads only in short sections. Mostly they lay even below the level of a German dirt track. Nevertheless, the ride through the high mountain landscape was on the whole delightful, both by day and at night, with the clear view of the mountains sharply outlined in the moonlight. In Maku there was no hotel, not even a teahouse. Our companions had brought beds for themselves, which they set up in the bus waiting room. For us, 2 benches were placed next to each other and a mattress laid on top, and so we had a quite comfortable bed on which we slept well. The next morning we saw the village, which is made up of mostly red-washed houses on a scree slope in a narrow valley beneath a mighty, vertical rock wall. We found ourselves at the bottom of the valley; above us the little houses clung up to about 100 m height like birds' nests, and then came the wall, which threatened to crush everything below it.

We did not have much to put on; for we had only taken off our jackets to sleep. There was no water for washing either, and brushing teeth was not even to be thought of. We again stuffed our bellies full with tea, bread, and cheese and drove on at 9 o'clock, now for the first time in the Chevrolet. As there was room for only 3 people, the chauffeur had to sit on the mudguard and the manager rode along squeezed in (German "fuhr eine Saunaht zusammen"; idiom unclear). Luckily it was only 4 km to Bazirgan, and the road was level. So in good weather we came to the two massive buildings at the outermost northwest tip of Iran, in which the police and customs are housed. Then there were a few sheds for goods and a few...

Then he asked me in good French where I wanted to go. I told him at length, and meanwhile Strauss appeared. Then we talked smoothly about the currency question and came to speak about the luggage. He himself had already exchanged everything into lira beforehand. Then together we enjoyed the magnificent view of Mount Ararat, which, at least according to the director, was supposed to have been very beautiful yesterday. We, however, tried in vain to penetrate with our gazes to the remains of Noah's Ark, for the summit of the mountain was wrapped in a black, impenetrable wall of fog. We then took leave of the director with mutual good wishes and wandered among the wreckage of the Turkish airplanes mentioned earlier, which are waiting there to be collected by the Turks. After that, we went with Abdil, the manager, into the village, where I sent off the telegram to you, beloved parents. Then Abdil invited us to lunch in his hut, which was made of mud and had 2 rooms. He lives there during his business in Bazirgan and wanted to stay there for some time now as well. The only furnishings were a samovar, a silou [mat], and a few cushions. The lunch, on the other hand, was excellent, consisting for the last time of genuine Iranian pilaf, roast chicken, potatoes, "hand towel" [flatbread], and grapes, which we ate squatting on the silou. There I also exchanged the rest of the money. Then we drove on between a few hills to the Turkish border village of Gürbulak (typed "Gürcübulak"), 4 km away. Nobody there spoke Persian, let alone any other language familiar to us. Communication therefore took place through the mediation of Ismael, our chauffeur. We had our money, the silver, and the collection entered in our passports, for there were no seals there. They took 2 brand-new tires from the chauffeur and searched the car down to the smallest detail. Meanwhile we strolled on the hills and painted indecent words in the fresh snow. At dark we went on to a larger town, about 20 km from the border, Bayazit. There we found an eatery with white-covered tables and menus; but we went into the kitchen and peeked into the pots, about 20 of which stood on the stove with various dishes. Then we pointed at what we wanted and so filled ourselves with well-prepared food. It had begun to snow and, as we drove on, the snow became ever denser. This car was a Ford that had no side windows anymore and was only makeshift covered with tarpaulin. In it we now drove on in the late afternoon, 5 persons. There were 2 trucks, the second a Chevrolet, on which Sträusschen's large luggage was. We wanted to cover barely 100 km and reach Karagossa. In the thick snow no road could be made out. Only 2 drifted-over ruts showed that something had driven along there some time ago. It was horribly cold as we set off. The heavy machines moved forward only at walking pace in first gear. The first 20 km went all right; for we needed only 2 hours for them. Then our Ford, which was driving in front, got stuck. It was an unpleasant situation in the ice-cold, clear, nocturnal mountains, deep snow as far as the eye could see. The chauffeur got every bit of power out of the engine, and the body shook under the strain as if it were about to burst at any moment. Nothing helped. Two men had to shovel the car free. Even then we only got a few hundred meters further, although the incline was slight. Again and again there had to be shoveling. Sometimes it looked hopeless; and then we had to get out too, to help push. Just getting out of the car, I sank in the snow to above my knees. The terribly cold air cut into the skin. I had wrapped my woolen shawl around my ears. After only a few seconds I could no longer breathe through my nose, since it had frozen shut. On the whole drive we had not yet come across a house or a person. Far and wide only mountains and vast amounts of snow. My feet were numb, even though on Sträusschen's advice I had pulled the mountain boots on over the thick colorful socks. Over my gloves I had also pulled a pair of stockings; but all that could not keep out the dreadful cold. I cursed the whole journey, America, and in particular human helplessness in the face of the forces of nature, and I would gladly have given a kingdom for a warm bed. But nobody took up this unique opportunity to become king. From a mountain height, while the others shoveled and we stomped about in the snow, we saw Ararat in the moonlight through the improbably clear night, a sight that would have thrilled us on a summer night. Then it went a few km downhill, all in first gear with full engine power; but at least it moved, until we got stuck again at the next rise. **Türkali, 3. January 1940.**

It is hard for me to bring myself to write. How endlessly depressing this decade is starting out for us. We are sitting here in a dark hole, a few stones piled on top of one another and stuck together with mud; the only furnishings are a small iron stove, a kerosene lamp, and 2 woven mats. The half-light comes from a window in the ceiling, 20 cm square, which is drifted over with snow. And the whole thing is in the high mountains in an unimaginably remote village. We had made a desperate attempt to cross the mountains and got stuck, miserably close to the pass, in meter-deep snow during a horrific snowstorm. After hours of exertion we left the sleds and luggage up there and rescued ourselves and the horses back to this dump. But I want to tell in order how it came to this!

The awakening in that lonely house took place on 25.12. of last year. It was a glorious sunny winter day, and when we stepped out in front of the house into the warm sun, the light-flooded snowy landscape before us, we were reconciled with our fate. Then we also saw, some distance away in the valley, a friendly village. We stomped without coats in the warm sun through the deep snow; but soon my nose froze shut. So I went into the first house to warm up. We were received very kindly and recognized from the lists lying around that we were at the village headman's. Communication was impossible. Nevertheless we conversed excellently with our hands for a while, during which we were served tea. Then we walked on, past a small park with the war memorial and the school, into an inn. Our chauffeurs were already sitting there, and we now learned that the village was called Taslicay, that it had been 35 degrees below zero in the night, and that we would now have to wait until gasoline came from Karagossa, 35 km away. The whole village came running to gape at us, among them one who spoke some Persian. He then took it upon himself to interpret for us. We went back once more to fetch our coats, the writing pad, and the playing cards, and then stayed in the inn until night. First we learned the Turkish numbers; then I made a list of the most important words that we needed constantly, and finally I began this report. You, dear Papa, will probably search in vain with your finger on the map. If you go up the Euphrates from Baghdad and, where it divides, follow the southern headstream, you finally come to Karagossa (Karaköse), and right at its beginning, where the Euphrates is as wide as the Klia, lies Taslicay.

Most of the day we spent playing cards. In between we were taken in by the lunch, which we left untouched because it was inedible. How good the Iranian rice was compared with this surrogate that they called rice. Good rice is very expensive in Turkey, and now we understood why our chauffeur, smirking contentedly, had paid 5 lire of duty at the border for 25 pounds of rice.

**7)** ...had paid. The evening meal we bought together: hazelnuts, raisins, apples, cheese, bread, and butter. The program of the next day did not differ significantly from the previous day, only that we waived lunch and Strauss was not mortally offended when he said I had a red beard. Otherwise we cursed and swore at this miserable delay; for back then we did not know that our health, perhaps even our lives, were saved by it. In the course of the afternoon the gasoline came, and the next morning it was to continue. We breathed sigh of relief, when we on Dec 27th sat in our cold Ford and drove off. The Chevrolet, which was 300m ahead of us, was already gone. The visibility in the hazy weather was miserable. Barely could one recognize the drifted ruts. So it happened that we got onto the spot where the Chevrolet had stood, in whose tracks stuck. During the monstrous exertions of the engine to get free, an axle broke, and with that it was completely over. We stomped disgruntled back into the village and rented through the mediation of the Persian-speaking person a sled. An hour later we loaded our luggage from the Ford onto the sled, except for the two large pieces of Strauss's, which were on the Chevrolet. Then we set off. I had my woolen scarf wrapped picturesquely around head and ears. Strauss had his ski cap, which was invaluable, and 2 left gloves; the right one he had lost on the way. Otherwise it was unthinkable uncomfortable. My feet froze in the mountain boots soon so that I could no longer move them. After a few kilometers the fat chauffeur of the Chevrolet came toward us; we stopped and he spoke excitedly with the driver. He gestured wild with both hands. As we drove further, we saw the mess. The car was a track off the road and overturned. The many boxes with goods lay peacefully with Strauss's suitcases scattered in the area. We loaded the two suitcases onto the sled and went further. At darkness we arrived in Karagossa, a larger village with electric lighting, according to the map the capital of the province Agri. In the hotel, Strauss pulled the shoes off my feet, which were completely frozen stiff. Under great pain I thawed out, while Strauss inspected our room and returned satisfied. Then we heard that the connection to Erzurum was impossible and went into the nearest kitchen to dine lavishly. As we returned to the hotel, we found barely a place. Only a few of those present consumed something. Most played "Puff" [backgammon], apparently the local national game; for anyone can do it and even children play it with ape-like speed. We soon went to our room, where it was freezing cold. Even when we lit the wooden logs in the stove, it became only temporarily insignificantly warmer. The beds were clean, and therefore I pulled off my clothes for the first time in a long time; but I could not get warm and so spent the night trying to get dressed again. I put on stockings and all the sweaters I had, put Strauss's ski cap on and tied my scarf over it; then I laid our two winter coats over me. When I finally fell asleep toward morning, it woke me up soon again facing the water pitcher, in which the water was frozen down to the bottom. Our morning toilet shaped up according to the cold and half-frozen we reached the guest room. Our 130 Toman were gone, we sat instead of in Trebizond in Karagossa and had to rely on ourselves alone. An unpleasant situation. Our day was filled with inquiries at the bus office when the road would be opened, targeting all chauffeurs, card and backgammon playing, eating, and in between I wrote on my report. The innkeepers of the hotel were charming. They were the only ones with whom we could communicate, as they spoke Persian and helped us in a touching way to get further. But the road was and remained closed for cars for an indefinite time, and there was no other way out than to decide for us on the 185km long ride on sleds.

**8)** Meanwhile we had tracked down our chauffeur on the street and dragged him into the hotel, having the innkeeper explain to him that we had paid up to Trebizond and made him responsible for the further journey. The 4 Lira for the sled ride from Taslicay he gave back to us and promised to care for the further journey to Erzurum. The horses did not look particularly trustworthy, but gave themselves honest effort. Their master had to strike them constantly to keep them at a trot. Nevertheless, we had often and long opportunity to walk at a pace; for every time we met someone on the way, the driver jumped down and held an extended little chat. Then he ran behind. And unfortunately we met many. So we had sufficient opportunity to enjoy the crisp air and watch the apple growth, which provided versatile topic of conversation for a while. After 35 km the day's goal was reached in a village named Alaskirt. Our luggage was unloaded and we ate roasted mutton legs, the only edible thing in the local kitchen. It was only 4 o'clock, but pitch dark. For that it becomes light at 6 o'clock in the morning. We sat in the village inn and had to let the usual staring at us happen, to which our driver gave the necessary explanations. Then we drank tea and thought of earlier New Year's Eves. My thoughts were with the loved ones in Shanghai; but I had only tea, which I could drink to Aunt Lottchen's health. We went to bed early. I woke up often from a scratching sound, which seemed to come from a cat. At 5 o'clock someone stomped into the chamber to wake us and then woke me a very faint "Prosit Neujahr, Fritz" [Happy New Year, Fritz] completely. Strauss, the poor guy, had barely slept; for the scratching sound came from water that constantly dripped onto his bed through the ceiling. The new decade thus let itself start quite unpleasantly.

At the first dawn of morning it went on in finely spraying rain. There were 3 sleds. 2 were added the evening before with a young Turk, his wife, 4 children, and luggage. Now it went into the feared mountains, constantly uphill, and we moved forward only at a walk. To our luck the rain stopped soon. Around 10 o'clock we had covered 22 km and stopped before the gendarmerie in Türkali, before us the high mountains, over which we had to go. The driver telephoned to Tahir, the day's goal, which is 18 km from here, and got word that the road was free. So we drove on. After a few hundred meters every trace lost itself in the high snow and we had to get out, because the horses could not pull the heavy sleds through. One went ahead to search for the path. If he sank only up to his knees in the snow, then we were on the right path. A heavy storm whipped us sharp ice crystals into the face, while we stomped uphill. Through the exertion we sweated despite the gruesome cold on the whole body. The higher we came, the sharper the storm became, the snow ever deeper. So we fought our way step-by-step forward for hours. As we were just before the ridge at a bend of the path, while I stomped ahead, I suddenly lay in the snow. The dreadful storm had blown me over. The horses stuck up to above the knee in snow. It was the end. We could not see 5m far. Beneath a gruesome howling the storm whipped the snow toward us. Added to this came the crying of the children from the covered sled. The driver and we both tried from top to bottom to make a path and vanished in the snowstorm, while we tried to get the horses free. Meanwhile the driver returned. Strauss remained vanished. Over an hour had passed and I made myself the greatest worries for him. People began to unharness the horses. Finally Strauss came back, completely out of breath. He was sunk up to his neck, had lost a rubber shoe in the process, and had to crawl back on his belly. Moving forward was not to be thought of. At the attempt to guide the horses past the sleds, one fell and rolled 30m deep down the slope. As one wanted to pull him up, it rolled even deeper...

I then came up with the idea of going to Athens and trying to catch the Citta di Bari there. We drove back to the travel agency, under police escort of course, and tried to reach Mr. G. Unfortunately that failed; but it was established that our ship would call at Piraeus the day after next and that we would reach it in time with the night train. The tickets were obtained, and we went with the policeman and our agent to fetch the luggage and then to the station, where we stored it. If we had now believed that we could finally get something to eat, we were mistaken, because our policeman brought us to the railway police, where they kept us. We were beside ourselves with nameless rage, because those pigs dared, in response to our protest, to lay hands on us and push us down onto a bench I wanted to call Mr. Goldenberg once more, but they did not allow that either. But they did fetch the station innkeeper, from whom we ordered food. At half past 10 at night, after 4 hours, we were brought to the station and were now supposed to check our large luggage. Then I explained to a German-speaking railway official that we had no money left and had not been allowed to telephone our uncle, who was supposed to settle it. It did us a lot of good to see the policeman get chewed out and have to allow us to put the suitcases in the corridor. We had a compartment of our own on the Orient Express, together with a policeman. As we pulled out, a large colorful poster laughed down at us with the inscription: Istanbul welcomes you! We had a different idea of welcome and were glad to get away from this hospitable city. In the morning the policeman left us, and at the next station we were in Pythion, Greece, where we had to change trains. There we fell into another faint, because the connecting train to Athens was gone, since our express was late, and the next train was not due to leave for 24 hours. It was now once again impossible to catch the Citta di Bari and with it the Conte di Savoia. We endured the customs inspection, had to have the large trunks and the stove sealed and checked, which cost a lot of money, and waited out the evening in that tiny hamlet. We spent the night in the waiting room and very early the next morning continued in a diesel railcar through all of Thrace, along the flooded plain of a wide river, where there were many cranes and other fowl. We rode via Syphilistraton, Syndetikon, Shaneion and whatever else they were called, to Alexandroupolis, where we had to change trains. This time we had a connection and got 2 window seats in an upholstered express-train compartment. Although the weather was unfriendly, the journey through Macedonia was beautiful. Here too there were many rocky mountains; but they were not as bare and desolate as in Iran and Turkey, but overgrown with green and with many little trees, with which they gave the snowy peaks in the background a gentle frame. Unfortunately there was as little tobacco to be seen now as there was of the eternally shining sun for which this landscape is world-famous. At 10 o'clock at night we were in Salonika, where our compartment filled up, so that we had to spend a very cramped night. Very early we looked out of the window again, enjoyed the charming landscape and made wise comparisons with regard to the historical background. Shortly past Thebes we then looked down into the valley onto Athens, with the port of Piraeus and the truly blue Mediterranean far in the background. We rode straight on to Piraeus to inquire about the next ship. At half past 11 we arrived and...

When we then came on deck, only a little of A[thens?] was still to be seen, and we glided in the most beautiful sunshine between the Greek islands. In the late afternoon we came to the Isthmus of Corinth and were towed by a small tug through the roughly 3km long, very narrow canal, which is a marvel of engineering. Then we reached the open sea. The next day, January 23, we were supposed to be in Brindisi at 12 o'clock, where a train left for Naples at 1. On the 24th the Conte di Savoia was to sail from Naples, and there was hope again that we would catch her. We were even in Brindisi an hour earlier than expected; but our passport and customs inspections took so long that we only got to the station 10 minutes before the train left. We took our tickets and, while Strauss brought the small luggage onto the train, I tried to check the two large trunks. The official in charge had once again gone off to breakfast, and in the meantime the train left. So there I sat alone in Brindisi and found in the timetable that I could take a train to Foggia at 3 o'clock, where I would have a connection to Naples at 6 and could get there before Strauss, who was taking the southern route. How it happened that the train then arrived in Foggia not shortly after 5 but only around 1[?] I still do not know today. Fortunately there was still a train after midnight, and so I landed in Naples at half past 6 in the morning, where Sträusschen was already waiting for me. We strolled through the streets, [which] had meanwhile become considerably cleaner. I [...] main post office [...], to telephone Berlin. I had been looking forward to it the whole trip and was very disappointed when the official said that Germany was not accepting calls from abroad. At 9 we went to the shipping company and just saw the C.d.S. come in. At noon sharp she sailed on with us and our belongings. We had an extremely poor outside cabin, against which the water struck with a thunderous crash. The only advantage was that we had it to ourselves. By far most of the passengers were emigrants coming from Germany. We joined some Hungarians with whom we ate at the same table, 2 ladies and a gentleman. Then we were together with some older couples from southern Germany and so soon had the long-awaited Skat game together. The day after next we came to Gibraltar in the most beautiful weather. It was a wonderful atmosphere: the peacefully lying and so harmless-looking Gibraltar, opposite it the charming landscape of southern Spain, and on the other side the long coast with the blue mountains of Africa. We lay there for 6 hours while the British authorities came aboard and left again. Not a trace of unrest was to be felt, and rightly so. The next day on the Atlantic I began to feel queasy. I then spent most of the time in the cabin, right on the edge of seasickness. On the day before our arrival I was on top again and tried to make up for lost time by eating my way up and down the menus. The sea was only lightly rippled as we approached the holy land. It was far more impressive than in all the cinemas put together when the skyscrapers of Manhattan rose out of the haze and we passed the huge bridges and new skyscrapers appeared after the first ones had disappeared again: an impressive end to our remarkable journey!
