**25. December 1939**

**Taslicay (Asia Minor)**

**My beloved parents!**

I, old butter-fingers, have of course forgotten once again to congratulate you on your 28th wedding anniversary, which I could have done so nicely with the telegram from Bazirgan on the 23rd of this month. Now I begin my travel report thinking of you both with heartfelt thoughts, with the earnest wish that we may celebrate today's day next year together with all our loved ones, and that you both may spend a carefree, pleasant time in best health in Tehran until your hopefully soon crossing!

Our farewell today 6 days ago came so suddenly, and everything I had still wanted to tell you remained stuck in my chest. With all the overcome and upcoming difficulties, we had had no time to think of the farewell, and that was good so. It was also good that everything then went so fast; for when the car picked us up and the separation thereby became a certainty, it hit us especially hard. It was a blessing that I could leave with the feeling of so many truly valuable friendships shown to me, of which I became particularly strongly aware in the last days before the departure. Even though this contributed a great deal to making the farewell so hard for me, I know for certain that you, beloved parents, are not alone! During the almost 2 hours that we still had to wait in the garage for the departure of the vehicle, where I hoped you might perhaps come by once more, I thought about the last 2 years, with the result of grateful contentment that I have gathered many vital experiences, found loyal friends, and not least was able to get you out of the immediate danger zone.

At a quarter to five the fourth fellow traveler finally arrived, an Armenian girl, with an enormous amount of luggage and provision baskets that would have been sufficient for our whole trip, and furthermore a host of sisters, brothers, and female friends. The third fellow traveler was a young man who somehow belonged to this girl and had waited full of impatience. Now the luggage had to be stowed, which did not happen without noisy differences of opinion and for which our things were first thrown out again. Finally, at 5 o'clock we drove off; the girl next to the chauffeur, we three men in the back, I in the middle. Vehicle and driver were excellent. We left Tehran in the twilight, I occupied with my retrospective thoughts. Hardly a word was spoken.

Even before Qazvin we ran into a light snowfall, which caused us mild concern. The desert was white, even if covered with only a very delicate layer of snow. At 7:30 we stopped in front of the "Grand Hotel" in Qazvin for a short rest. Strauss had spent 4 days there on his journey to Tehran and, in a surge of joy at the reunion, rushed to the venerable radio set, without noteworthy success. Meanwhile, I ordered borscht and tea. We still wanted to save our sandwiches. On account of the affluent impression we made, they took 8 Kran from us for this meager snack. Then we went on through the snowfall to Zanjan, where we arrived around midnight. We were very tired and requested a room in the only hotel of the place. Most rooms were locked and the key could not be found. After the girl and the boy were accommodated, a room could then be forced open for us as well, though with only one bed. As it was bitterly cold, that seemed quite favorable to us, and we lay down. I was already lying down when the boy...

From snow-white landscapes we came quite suddenly into completely snowless ones, and vice versa. The girl had apparently, unlike us, eaten a hearty breakfast, and now began to vomit incessantly. Once again I had reason to thank fate that I sat in the back. At 10 o'clock we made a tea break in a little village named Taserkand, i.e. we both drank delicious warm milk. Lunch was in Mianeh (typed "Mayimäh"?), after we had crossed a high, heavily snowed mountain range, unlike many other cars that had gotten stuck in the snow. We ate good, cheap roast chicken with fried potatoes and then drove on, until Strauss and I suddenly both jumped up at the same time. For there, in front of a standalone teahouse, exactly 100 km before Tabriz, stood the bus with our luggage, which had already been on the road for 4 days. After Strauss had urged me at every difficult spot on the road to check whether I could still gather up a few of my postage stamps, this was finally a certain reassurance for me. Later it went in endless serpentines over a high mountain wrapped in clouds, where the chauffeur showed us the spot where, after the Crown Prince's wedding, 2 Turkish airplanes flew into the mountain and crashed, and at 6:30 we were in Tabriz.

Hamid-Aga, the garage owner to whom Mr. Fischer had recommended us, explained to us over tea that on Thursday a truck was to leave for Trebizond [Trabzon]. He would try to get us a place on it. We then took quarters in the Grand Hotel and got the allegedly last room, which fully satisfied our expectations, cleaned ourselves up, and decided to go to the cinema. There we then slept quite well, and continued doing so later in the hotel. The next morning the luggage was there, and in the garage I first changed my shoes, which had been giving me a hard time. Then another discussion with Hamid-Aga. Communication was miserable, so he brought us to a pharmacy opposite, where a sympathetic older gentleman interpreted via French. Strauss mentioned in doing so that we were Jewish emigrants, whereupon he introduced himself as a Lithuanian Jew and helped us in a touching way. He walked with us to his friend, a transport expert, and then sent us with his assistant into the bazaar, where we settled the trip to Trebizond for 130 Toman with the manager of a firm.

It was a 1.5-ton Chevrolet with room for 3 persons. Our luggage was loaded right away; we were to go as far as Bazirgan by bus, since the manager was riding in the truck until there. We then ate an excellent portion of partridge in the hotel, which filled both of us up despite our huge appetite. Then we went into the bazaar and immediately found Mr. Fischer's acquaintance, a black-framed moon face squatting cross-legged, asked for his English-speaking son, who was fetched for us at once, and acquired "bx" for "tl." (abbreviations unclear; probably a currency exchange). Then we said goodbye to Isaac Rabinowitsch, the pharmacist, and went to the bus garage. The bus was supposed to leave at 3 o'clock; but by then it had become 4:15, and we still had to wait another hour. For a few Kran the best seats had been reserved for us. The bus was stuffed full. People were even squatting packed tightly in the aisle. We drove into the night, at first along the railway line to Julfa, which we crossed many times. Our goal for this day was Khoi. In a small village halfway there, a supper stop was made. We drank tea in vast quantities and watched the men praying. That made us hungry and we ordered bread and cheese, since our provisions had meanwhile run out. There we received a few "hand towels" [sheets of flatbread] of the best quality and sheep's cheese, which we really...

We then pounded for 10 minutes on the door of the local Esplanade [hotel] with our belongings, and in this way alerted the policeman on duty, while in the hotel not a soul stirred. We were, however, not arrested for disturbing the peace, as was to be expected; instead, the sergeant helped produce further din, which then led to success. We were given the two-bed deluxe room; but we crept back together into one bed and slept quite well through the night. The next morning we strolled through the bazaar, where we each bought a pair of thick, colorful wool socks for 3 Kran, which actually look more like slippers and are worn by almost all people there. For lunch we drank excellent dugh [sour milk drink] and ate good kebab with it, and at 3 o'clock we drove on with an ancient bus that was mainly loaded with junk. We were only 7 people, and it went over high mountains. Slower than a pedestrian, the old box wheezed laboriously up in first gear, and every 2 km it was stopped to refill large amounts of carried water into the boiling radiator. Downhill it went fast, and finally came a broad river, along which we drove upstream for a stretch, to then stop in a small village at dusk. We drank tea in a picturesque opium den, scraped together a few more of your pistachios, Junilein, from the bottom of the briefcase, and then drove further into the dark mountains. About 10 km before Maku another stop was made, and there stood our Chevrolet again too. We were greeted noisily by the two and dragged into a dive where we had supper: "hand towels" of lesser quality, excellent mast [yogurt], and a porridge made of flour, sugar, and mutton fat. We tore a rag off the hand towel, packed a piece of cheese, mast, or porridge on it with our fingers, and rolled it up like a giant cigarette. Then we wolfed down the whole thing, and because we were hungry it tasted excellent. At 11 o'clock we arrived in Maku.

**Karagossa, 28.12.39.**

From Tabriz on, one could call the paths roads only in short sections. Mostly they lay even below the level of a German dirt track. Nevertheless, the ride through the high mountain landscape was on the whole delightful, both by day and at night, with the clear view of the mountains sharply outlined in the moonlight. In Maku there was no hotel, not even a teahouse. Our companions had brought beds for themselves, which they set up in the bus waiting room. For us, 2 benches were placed next to each other and a mattress laid on top, and so we had a quite comfortable bed on which we slept well. The next morning we saw the village, which is made up of mostly red-washed houses on a scree slope in a narrow valley beneath a mighty, vertical rock wall. We found ourselves at the bottom of the valley; above us the little houses clung up to about 100 m height like birds' nests, and then came the wall, which threatened to crush everything below it.

We did not have much to put on; for we had only taken off our jackets to sleep. There was no water for washing either, and brushing teeth was not even to be thought of. We again stuffed our bellies full with tea, bread, and cheese and drove on at 9 o'clock, now for the first time in the Chevrolet. As there was room for only 3 people, the chauffeur had to sit on the mudguard and the manager rode along squeezed in (German "fuhr eine Saunaht zusammen"; idiom unclear). Luckily it was only 4 km to Bazirgan, and the road was level. So in good weather we came to the two massive buildings at the outermost northwest tip of Iran, in which the police and customs are housed. Then there were a few sheds for goods and a few...

**4)** ...a hundred meters further to the left, in the shelter of the mountains, the village of Bazirgan. Even before, the chauffeur had told us that the customs director had 2 bad teeth that plagued him terribly. I dreaded any bad moods of the mighty man, and now I had to watch in dismay as a horde of porters [hamals] rushed at the car, took down our luggage, and dragged it into the cellar of the customs building. Then I pulled myself together and climbed the stairs up to the office. Behind a desk sat a stocky, graying gentleman in uniform, to whom I held out my hand with an unselfconscious "Salam aleikum", which he politely took. Then he asked me in good French where I wanted to go. I told him at length, and meanwhile Strauss appeared. Then we talked smoothly about the currency question and came to speak about the luggage. I laboriously dug out my various certificates. Meanwhile, the servants dragged our suitcases in, so that the small room became so full that one could barely turn around. We unlocked the suitcases, and while an assistant of the chief set about a thorough search, the director consulted us about his teeth. In the absence of instruments and medicines we most obligingly gave home-remedy advice and looked at the aching teeth with an important expression. In between, the henchman brought Sträusschen's photo album, which seemed suspicious to him, and we admired the pictures together, while the other opened my large suitcase. When he was about to dig deep there too and had just reached my photo album, which he brought over, the mighty man, with the already-answered question to me "These are only your personal effects in there!?", ordered the suitcases closed. Then the director remarked that we could not exchange our 20 Toman in Turkey anyway, and produced an Iraqi [1?] note, which I acquired for 10 Toman. Spurred on by the good example, the assistant also brought 7 Turkish lira, for which I gave him 14 Toman. That left me with only a little over 10 Toman, which Strauss was holding for me. He himself had already exchanged everything into lira beforehand. Then together we enjoyed the magnificent view of Mount Ararat, which, at least according to the director, was supposed to have been very beautiful yesterday. We, however, tried in vain to penetrate with our gazes to the remains of Noah's Ark, for the summit of the mountain was wrapped in a black, impenetrable wall of fog. We then took leave of the director with mutual good wishes and wandered among the wreckage of the Turkish airplanes mentioned earlier, which are waiting there to be collected by the Turks. After that, we went with Abdil, the manager, into the village, where I sent off the telegram to you, beloved parents. Then Abdil invited us to lunch in his hut, which was made of mud and had 2 rooms. He lives there during his business in Bazirgan and wanted to stay there for some time now as well. The only furnishings were a samovar, a silou [mat], and a few cushions. The lunch, on the other hand, was excellent, consisting for the last time of genuine Iranian pilaf, roast chicken, potatoes, "hand towel" [flatbread], and grapes, which we ate squatting on the silou. There I also exchanged the rest of the money. Then we drove on between a few hills to the Turkish border village of Gürbulak (typed "Gürcübulak"), 4 km away. Nobody there spoke Persian, let alone any other language familiar to us. Communication therefore took place through the mediation of Ismael, our chauffeur. We had our money, the silver, and the collection entered in our passports, for there were no seals there. They took 2 brand-new tires from the chauffeur and searched the car down to the smallest detail. Meanwhile we strolled on the hills and painted indecent words in the fresh snow. At dark we went on to a larger town, about 20 km from the border, Bayazit. There we found an eatery with white-covered tables and menus; but we went into the kitchen and peeked into the pots, about 20 of which stood on the stove with various dishes. Then we pointed at what we wanted and so filled ourselves with well-prepared food. It had begun to snow and, as we drove on, the snow became ever denser. After another 25 km we arrived in a small, deeply snowed-in village, "Kizildice". There was only a miserable dive there with a small side room, in which 10 plank beds...

**5)** ...were. It was dark, cramped, and bitterly cold. We only took off our jackets and slept quite well even under these circumstances; for we were dog-tired. Unwashed, frozen, and hungry, we sat in the dive the next morning. There was nothing to eat. We drank bad tea and waited to move on. But the chauffeurs made no move to drive on, because the road was snowed in. We tried to go for a walk, but turned back immediately because it was too cold. So we pulled out the cards and played 66 up to a hundred thousand, and when we were done with that, we started over again. In the afternoon we could no longer stand the hunger. They brought us, in a tin bowl, a slop of unappetizing chunks of meat with even more unappetizing egg over it. We pounced on it like wolves, even though the sight alone would normally have made us retch. It was just as well that the stuff tasted only of salt; but nevertheless we left it after the first bites, whereupon the other guests took care of it. Then we played on until our chauffeur came and told us that he had to go back to Gürbulak because of the tires. We would continue in another truck, for which he had arranged everything.

This car was a Ford that had no side windows anymore and was only makeshift covered with tarpaulin. In it we now drove on in the late afternoon, 5 persons. There were 2 trucks, the second a Chevrolet, on which Sträusschen's large luggage was. We wanted to cover barely 100 km and reach Karagossa. In the thick snow no road could be made out. Only 2 drifted-over ruts showed that something had driven along there some time ago. It was horribly cold as we set off. The heavy machines moved forward only at walking pace in first gear. The first 20 km went all right; for we needed only 2 hours for them. Then our Ford, which was driving in front, got stuck. It was an unpleasant situation in the ice-cold, clear, nocturnal mountains, deep snow as far as the eye could see. The chauffeur got every bit of power out of the engine, and the body shook under the strain as if it were about to burst at any moment. Nothing helped. Two men had to shovel the car free. Even then we only got a few hundred meters further, although the incline was slight. Again and again there had to be shoveling. Sometimes it looked hopeless; and then we had to get out too, to help push. Just getting out of the car, I sank in the snow to above my knees. The terribly cold air cut into the skin. I had wrapped my woolen shawl around my ears. After only a few seconds I could no longer breathe through my nose, since it had frozen shut. On the whole drive we had not yet come across a house or a person. Far and wide only mountains and vast amounts of snow. My feet were numb, even though on Sträusschen's advice I had pulled the mountain boots on over the thick colorful socks. Over my gloves I had also pulled a pair of stockings; but all that could not keep out the dreadful cold. I cursed the whole journey, America, and in particular human helplessness in the face of the forces of nature, and I would gladly have given a kingdom for a warm bed. But nobody took up this unique opportunity to become king. From a mountain height, while the others shoveled and we stomped about in the snow, we saw Ararat in the moonlight through the improbably clear night, a sight that would have thrilled us on a summer night. Then it went a few km downhill, all in first gear with full engine power; but at least it moved, until we got stuck again at the next rise. One of our shovelers came back after a few minutes howling like a child and then collapsed. We tore the rags off his feet, rubbed them with snow, dried them with my blanket, and put on the boy the stockings that I had been wearing over my gloves. Then we prepared ourselves to spend the night in the open car, five of us crammed together, a delightful prospect, especially since hunger tormented us and we had nothing with us and had not eaten anything for 24 hours either. It was still 40 km to Karagossa. Meanwhile the lighter Chevrolet had squeezed past us, and so we tried once more, actually got free, and followed...

**Türkali, 3. January 1940.**

It is hard for me to bring myself to write. How endlessly depressing this decade is starting out for us. We are sitting here in a dark hole, a few stones piled on top of one another and stuck together with mud; the only furnishings are a small iron stove, a kerosene lamp, and 2 woven mats. The half-light comes from a window in the ceiling, 20 cm square, which is drifted over with snow. And the whole thing is in the high mountains in an unimaginably remote village. We had made a desperate attempt to cross the mountains and got stuck, miserably close to the pass, in meter-deep snow during a horrific snowstorm. After hours of exertion we left the sleds and luggage up there and rescued ourselves and the horses back to this dump. But I want to tell in order how it came to this!

The awakening in that lonely house took place on 25.12. of last year. It was a glorious sunny winter day, and when we stepped out in front of the house into the warm sun, the light-flooded snowy landscape before us, we were reconciled with our fate. Then we also saw, some distance away in the valley, a friendly village. We stomped without coats in the warm sun through the deep snow; but soon my nose froze shut. So I went into the first house to warm up. We were received very kindly and recognized from the lists lying around that we were at the village headman's. Communication was impossible. Nevertheless we conversed excellently with our hands for a while, during which we were served tea. Then we walked on, past a small park with the war memorial and the school, into an inn. Our chauffeurs were already sitting there, and we now learned that the village was called Taslicay, that it had been 35 degrees below zero in the night, and that we would now have to wait until gasoline came from Karagossa, 35 km away. The whole village came running to gape at us, among them one who spoke some Persian. He then took it upon himself to interpret for us. We went back once more to fetch our coats, the writing pad, and the playing cards, and then stayed in the inn until night. First we learned the Turkish numbers; then I made a list of the most important words that we needed constantly, and finally I began this report. You, dear Papa, will probably search in vain with your finger on the map. If you go up the Euphrates from Baghdad and, where it divides, follow the southern headstream, you finally come to Karagossa (Karaköse), and right at its beginning, where the Euphrates is as wide as the Klia, lies Taslicay.

Most of the day we spent playing cards. In between we were taken in by the lunch, which we left untouched because it was inedible. How good the Iranian rice was compared with this surrogate that they called rice. Good rice is very expensive in Turkey, and now we understood why our chauffeur, smirking contentedly, had paid 5 lire of duty at the border for 25 pounds of rice.

**7)** We bought our supper together: hazelnuts, raisins, apples, cheese, bread, and butter. The program of the next day did not differ significantly from the previous day's, except that we did without lunch and Strauss mortally offended me by saying that I had a red beard. Otherwise we cursed and swore at this miserable delay; for back then we did not yet know that our health, perhaps even our lives, were saved by it. For without delays we would have landed with astonishing precision right in the middle of the earthquake.

In the course of the afternoon the gasoline came, and the next morning we were to continue. We audibly breathed a sigh of relief when, on 27 December, we sat in our cold Ford and drove off. The Chevrolet, which had got stuck 300m ahead of us, was already gone. The visibility in the hazy weather was miserable. One could barely make out the drifted-over ruts. So it happened that, at the spot where the Chevrolet had stood, we got stuck in its tracks. During the monstrous exertions of the engine to get free, a rear axle broke, and with that it was all over. We stomped disgruntled back into the village and, through the mediation of the Persian speaker, rented a sled. An hour later we loaded our luggage from the Ford onto the sled, except for Strauss's two large pieces, which were on the Chevrolet. Then we set off. I had wrapped my woolen shawl picturesquely around my head and ears. Strauss had his ski cap, which was invaluable, and 2 left gloves; the right ones he had lost on the way. Otherwise it was as uncomfortable as could be. My feet soon froze in my mountain boots, so that I could no longer move them. After a few kilometers the fat chauffeur of the Chevrolet came toward us; we stopped and he spoke excitedly with the driver. He gestured wildly at us with both hands. As we drove on, we saw the mess. The car had gone a little off the road and overturned. The many sacks of goods lay peacefully scattered about the area together with Sträusschen's suitcases. We loaded the two suitcases onto the sled and drove on. At nightfall we arrived in Karagossa, a larger village with electric lighting, according to the map the capital of the province of Agri. In the hotel Strauss pulled the shoes off my feet, which were completely frozen stiff. With great pain I thawed them out, while Strauss inspected our room and came back satisfied. Then we heard that the connection to Erzurum was impossible, and went to the nearest kitchen to dine lavishly. When we returned to the hotel, we found barely a place. Only a few of those present were consuming anything. Most were playing "Puff" [backgammon], apparently the local national game; for everyone can play it, and even children play it with ape-like speed. We soon went to our room, where it was shockingly cold. Even when we lit the wooden logs in the stove, it became only temporarily and insignificantly warmer. The beds were clean, and so for the first time in a long while I undressed; but I could not get warm, and so I spent the night getting dressed again. I put on stockings and every sweater I had, put on Sträusschen's ski cap and tied my shawl over it; then I also laid our two winter coats over me. When I had finally fallen asleep toward morning, I was soon woken by Sträusschen's scornful laughter at the sight of me, which, however, quickly froze again at the sight of the water carafe, in which the water was frozen to the bottom. Our morning toilette was in keeping with the cold, and half-frozen we reached the guest room. We were rid of our 130 Toman, sat in Karagossa instead of Trebizond, and knew that we had to rely on ourselves alone. An unpleasant situation. Our day was filled with inquiries at the bus office about when the road would be clear, sounding out every chauffeur, playing cards and backgammon, gorging, and in between I wrote my report. The innkeepers of the hotel were charming. They were the only ones with whom we could communicate, for they spoke Persian, and they helped us in a touching way to get further. But the road was and remained closed to cars for an indefinite time, and there was no other way out than to decide on the 185km journey by sled.

**8)** Meanwhile we had picked up our chauffeur in the street, dragged him into the hotel, and had the innkeeper explain to him that we had paid as far as Trebizond and held him responsible for the onward journey. He gave us back the 4 Lira for the sled ride from Taslicay and promised to take care of the onward journey to Erzurum. But when one day after another passed and nothing moved, we set off toward Erzurum on the morning of 31 December of last year, after we had squeezed half the cost of the sled ride, 9 Lira, out of the chauffeur. The horses did not look particularly trustworthy, but made an honest effort. Their master had to beat them constantly to keep them at a trot. Nevertheless they often had long opportunities to go at a walk; for every time we met someone on the way, the driver jumped down and held an extended little chat. Then he came running after us. And unfortunately we met many people. So we had ample opportunity to enjoy the bracing air and watch the apple growth, which provided varied topics of conversation for a while. After 35 km the day's goal was reached, in a village named Alaskirt. Our luggage was unloaded and we ate roasted legs of mutton, the only edible thing in the local kitchen. It was only 4 o'clock, but pitch dark. In return it becomes light at 6 o'clock in the morning. We sat in the village inn and had to put up with the usual staring, for which our driver gave the necessary explanations. Then we drank tea and thought of earlier New Year's Eves. My thoughts were with the loved ones in Shanghai; but I had only tea, which I could drink to Aunt Lottchen's health. We went to bed early. I often woke up from a scratching sound that seemed to come from a cat. At 5 o'clock someone stomped into the chamber to wake us, and then a very feeble "Prosit Neujahr, Fritz" [Happy New Year, Fritz] woke me completely. Strauss, the poor fellow, had barely slept; for the scratching sound came from water that had been dripping constantly through the ceiling onto his bed. The new decade continued to start quite unpleasantly. At the first dawn we went on in a fine drizzling rain. There were 3 sleds. 2 had joined us the evening before, with a young Turk, his wife, 4 children, and luggage. Now we went into the dreaded mountains, constantly uphill, and we moved forward only at a walk. Luckily the rain soon stopped. Around 10 o'clock we had covered 22 km and stopped in front of the gendarmerie in Türkali, before us the high mountains which we had to cross. The driver telephoned to Tahir, the day's goal, which is 18 km from here, and got word that the road was clear. So we drove on. After a few hundred meters every trace was lost in the deep snow and we had to get out, because the horses could not pull the heavy sleds through. One man went ahead to find the path. If he sank into the snow only up to his knees, then we were on the right path. A violent storm whipped sharp ice crystals into our faces as we stomped uphill. From the exertion we sweated all over our bodies despite the gruesome cold. The higher we came, the sharper the storm became, the snow ever deeper. So for hours we fought our way forward step by step. When, shortly before the ridge, we came around a bend in the path while I was stomping ahead, I suddenly lay in the snow. The dreadful storm had blown me over. The horses were stuck in the snow up to above their knees. It was the end. We could not see 5m ahead. With a gruesome howling the storm whipped the snow against us. Added to this came the crying of the children from the covered sled. The drivers and we two were covered from top to bottom with a crust of ice. One of the drivers and Strauss tried to find a way and vanished in the snowstorm, while we tried to get the horses free. Meanwhile the driver came back. Strauss remained missing. Over an hour had passed and I was extremely worried about him. They began to unharness the horses. Finally Strauss came back, completely out of breath. He had sunk in up to his neck, had lost a rubber overshoe in the process, and had to crawl back on his belly. Moving forward was out of the question. In the attempt to lead the horses back past the sleds, one fell and rolled 3m down the slope. When they tried to pull it up, it rolled ever deeper.

**9)** The situation was desperate. The father of the family had meanwhile crawled out from under the cover and drew a revolver, with which he fired twice into the air. The bang died away in the storm. For the fallen horse a path was dug to the side, along which they managed to get it back up. Sträusschen and I just looked at each other uncomprehendingly, until I asked: What for? There was no answer to that; but after a while he added that the penalty was being waived because it was the first time. So, probation! It cost another 2 lira for the policeman to ride with us to the bank, where I got my check shortly before closing time.

**17)** While we were being brought back to the police station, the agent fetched the Italian visas. Then they took us to the main police station to collect the exit visas. Our hopes of still catching the Citta di Bari faded. The agent went to the harbor to delay the departure, and it became 2 o'clock. At 3 he came back and said that the ship could not wait any longer. By half past 5 we finally had not only the exit visas but also the expulsion order to leave the country that same day.

I then came up with the idea of going to Athens and trying to catch the Citta di Bari there. We drove back to the travel agency, under police escort of course, and tried to reach Mr. G. Unfortunately that failed; but it was established that our ship would call at Piraeus the day after next and that we would reach it in time with the night train. The tickets were obtained, and we went with the policeman and our agent to fetch the luggage and then to the station, where we stored it. If we had now believed that we could finally get something to eat, we were mistaken, because our policeman brought us to the railway police, where they kept us. We were beside ourselves with nameless rage, because those pigs dared, in response to our protest, to lay hands on us and push us down onto a bench I wanted to call Mr. Goldenberg once more, but they did not allow that either. But they did fetch the station innkeeper, from whom we ordered food. At half past 10 at night, after 4 hours, we were brought to the station and were now supposed to check our large luggage. Then I explained to a German-speaking railway official that we had no money left and had not been allowed to telephone our uncle, who was supposed to settle it. It did us a lot of good to see the policeman get chewed out and have to allow us to put the suitcases in the corridor. We had a compartment of our own on the Orient Express, together with a policeman. As we pulled out, a large colorful poster laughed down at us with the inscription: Istanbul welcomes you! We had a different idea of welcome and were glad to get away from this hospitable city. In the morning the policeman left us, and at the next station we were in Pythion, Greece, where we had to change trains. There we fell into another faint, because the connecting train to Athens was gone, since our express was late, and the next train was not due to leave for 24 hours. It was now once again impossible to catch the Citta di Bari and with it the Conte di Savoia. We endured the customs inspection, had to have the large trunks and the stove sealed and checked, which cost a lot of money, and waited out the evening in that tiny hamlet. We spent the night in the waiting room and very early the next morning continued in a diesel railcar through all of Thrace, along the flooded plain of a wide river, where there were many cranes and other fowl. We rode via Syphilistraton, Syndetikon, Shaneion and whatever else they were called, to Alexandroupolis, where we had to change trains. This time we had a connection and got 2 window seats in an upholstered express-train compartment. Although the weather was unfriendly, the journey through Macedonia was beautiful. Here too there were many rocky mountains; but they were not as bare and desolate as in Iran and Turkey, but overgrown with green and with many little trees, with which they gave the snowy peaks in the background a gentle frame. Unfortunately there was as little tobacco to be seen now as there was of the eternally shining sun for which this landscape is world-famous. At 10 o'clock at night we were in Salonika, where our compartment filled up, so that we had to spend a very cramped night. Very early we looked out of the window again, enjoyed the charming landscape and made wise comparisons with regard to the historical background. Shortly past Thebes we then looked down into the valley onto Athens, with the port of Piraeus and the truly blue Mediterranean far in the background. We rode straight on to Piraeus to inquire about the next ship. At half past 11 we arrived and...

How it happened that the train then arrived in Foggia not shortly after 5 but only around 1[?] I still do not know today. Fortunately there was still a train after midnight, and so I landed in Naples at half past 6 in the morning, where Sträusschen was already waiting for me. We strolled through the streets, [which] had meanwhile become considerably cleaner. I [...] main post office [...], to telephone Berlin. I had been looking forward to it the whole trip and was very disappointed when the official said that Germany was not accepting calls from abroad. We had an extremely poor outside cabin, against which the water struck with a thunderous crash. The only advantage was that we had it to ourselves. By far most of the passengers were emigrants coming from Germany. We joined some Hungarians with whom we ate at the same table, 2 ladies and a gentleman. It was far more impressive than in all the cinemas put together when the skyscrapers of Manhattan rose out of the haze and we passed the huge bridges and new skyscrapers appeared after the first ones had disappeared again: an impressive end to our remarkable journey!
