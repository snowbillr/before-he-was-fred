**25. December 1939**

**Taslicay (Asia Minor)**

**My beloved parents!**

I, old butter-fingers, have of course forgotten once again to congratulate you on your 28th wedding anniversary, which I could have done so nicely with the telegram from Bazirgan on the 23rd. Would that I had! Now I begin my travel report thinking of you both with heartfelt thoughts, with the earnest wish that we may celebrate today's day in the coming year together with all our loved ones, and that you both may spend a carefree, pleasant time in best health in Tehran until your hopefully soon departure!

Our farewell today 6 days ago came so suddenly, and everything I had still wanted to tell you remained stuck in my throat. With all the overcome and upcoming difficulties, we had no time to think of the farewell, and that was good so. It was also good that everything went so fast; for when the car picked us up and the separation thereby only became a certainty, it hit us especially hard. It was a blessing that I became particularly strongly aware in the last days before the departure of the feeling of so many truly valuable friendships shown to me, for if anything helped to make the farewell so hard for me, I know that you, beloved parents, are not alone! While we had to wait almost 2 ½ hours for the departure of the vehicle in the garage, where I hoped you might come by once more, I thought about the last 2 years with the result of deep gratitude for having gathered many vital experiences, found loyal friends, and not least, being able to escape the immediate danger zone.

Around 4:30 / 5:00, the fourth fellow traveler finally arrived: an Armenian girl, with an enormous amount of luggage and provision baskets that were more than sufficient for our trip, though meant for others; furthermore, a host of sisters, brothers, and female friends. The third fellow traveler was a young man who somehow belonged to this girl and had waited with full impatience. Now the luggage had to be stowed, which did not happen without noisy differences of opinion and for which our things were first thrown out again. Finally, at 5 o'clock we drove off; the chauffeur, we three men, I in the middle, in the back. Vehicle and driver were excellent. We left Tehran in the twilight; I was barely occupied with my backward-looking thoughts. Spoken was hardly a word.

Even before Qazvin we ran into a light snowfall, which caused mild concern in us. The plain was white, even if covered with only a very delicate layer of snow. At 8:30 we stopped in front of the "Grand Hotel" in Qazvin for a short rest. Strauss had spent 4 days there on his journey to Tehran and rushed in remarkable joy of reunion to the venerable radio set, without noteworthy success. Meanwhile, I ordered bread and tea. Our bread was meant to spare that of the affluent Landrock, which we had taken along; he took 8 Toman from us for the meager snack. Then we went on through the snowfall toward Zanjan, where we arrived around midnight. We were very tired and requested a room in the only hotel of the place. Most rooms were locked and the key could not be found. After the girl and the young man were accommodated, they let us in by force into a room as well, though with only one bed. As it was bitterly cold, two rooms seemed unsuitable, and we lay down together. I was already asleep when the young man...

At 10 o'clock we made a tea break in a village named Taserkand, after we both drank delicious hot milk. Noon found us in Mianeh; it was a high, heavily snowed mountain range we had crossed. In contrast to many other cars that were stuck in the snow, we ate good and cheap roast chicken with fried potatoes and then drove on, until Strauss and I suddenly stepped into the middle of the road at the same time. For there stood, in front of a standalone teahouse, exactly 100 km before Tabriz, the bus with our luggage, which had already been on the road for 4 days. After Strauss had challenged me at every difficult spot on the road to check whether I could gather a few of my postage stamps, this was finally a certain reassurance for me. Later it went in endless serpentines over a high mountain, wrapped in clouds, where our chauffeur pointed out the spot to us where, after the Crown Prince's wedding, 2 Turkish airplanes flew against the mountain and crashed; and at 5:30 we were in Tabriz.

Hamid-Aga, the garage owner to whom Mr. Fischer had recommended us, explained to us over tea that on Thursday a truck would leave for Trebizond [Trabzon] that wanted to take us. He would try to accommodate us there. We then moved into the Grand Hotel as quarters and got the allegedly last room, which fully satisfied our expectations. We ate and decided to go to the cinema; there we slept quite well and then continued sleeping in the hotel later. The next morning the luggage was there and I changed my shoes in the garage first, as they had pinched me severely. Then came a discussion with Hamid-Aga. Communication was miserable, so he brought us to a pharmacy opposite, where a sympathetic older gentleman translated via French. Strauss mentioned in doing so that we were Jewish emigrants, whereupon he proved to be a Lithuanian Jew and was helpful to us in a touching way. He ran with us to his friend, a transport specialist, and then sent us with his clerk into the bazaar, where we made the trip to Trebizond perfect with the manager of a firm for 130 Toman.

It was a 1.5-ton Chevrolet with space for 5 persons. Our luggage was loaded immediately; we were to ride to Bazirgan by bus, since the manager was riding in the truck until then. We then ate a portion of roast chicken in the hotel, which filled both of us up despite a huge appetite. Then we went into the bazaar and immediately found Mr. Fischer's acquaintance, a man sitting with crossed legs, a black-framed moon face, asked for his English-speaking son, who was brought to us right away and negotiated the bus for 1 Toman for us. Then we said goodbye to Isaac Rabinowitsch, the pharmacist, and went to the bus garage. The bus was supposed to leave at 3 o'clock; but by then it had become 4:15, and we still had to wait an hour. Space had been reserved for us for a few Toman—the best seats. The bus was stuffed full. People were even sitting packed tightly in the aisle. We drove into the night, initially along the railway line to Julfa, which we crossed many times. Our goal for this day was Khoi. In a small courtyard along the way, an evening stop was made. We drank tea in rough crowds and watched the men praying. From that we got hungry and ordered bread and cheese, since our provisions had meanwhile gone bad. We received a few hand towels of firmer quality and sheep's cheese, which we really...

For lunch we drank excellent sour milk (*dough*) and ate good kebab with it, and at 3 o'clock we drove with an ancient bus, which was mainly loaded with high piles of junk, further. We were only 7 people and it went up over high mountains. Slower than a pedestrian, the bus chugged up the first slope, and every 2 km it was stopped to refill large amounts of carried water into the boiling radiator. Downhill it went fast, and finally a broader river, in which we drove a piece upstream, to then make a stop in a small village at dusk. We drank tea in a picturesque opium den, rooted out a few pistachios from Iran, dried from the floor of the folder, and then drove further into the dark mountains. About 10 km before Maku another stop was made, and there stood our Chevrolet again. We were greeted by the two drivers with noise and dragged into a dive bar where we ate dinner: hand-towels of lesser quality, an excellent dish and a porridge made of flour, sugar, and mutton fat. We ripped a rag off the hand-towel, packed a piece of cheese, fat, or porridge on top with our fingers, rolled it up like a giant cigarette, and ate the whole thing; and because we were hungry it tasted excellent. At 11 o'clock we arrived in Maku.

**Karagossa, 28.12.39.**

From Tabriz to Maku, one could only describe the paths in short sections as roads. Mostly they lay below the level of a German dirt road. Nevertheless, the ride through the high mountain landscape was overall delightful, both by day and by night under clear visibility in the sharp moonlight against the mountains. In Maku there was no hotel, not even a teahouse. Our companions had beds for themselves, which they set up in the bus waiting room. For us, 2 benches were placed next to each other, a mattress laid on top, and so we had a very comfortable bed on which we slept well.

The next morning we saw the village, which sits in a narrow valley under a mighty, vertical rock wall on a scree slope made mostly of red-tinted houses. We found ourselves at the bottom of the valley; above us clung the houses up to 100m height like birds' nests, and then came the wall, which threatened to crush everything below it.

There was not much to attract us; for we had taken off our jackets to sleep, water for washing was non-existent, and brushing teeth was not even to be thought of. We stuffed our bellies full with tea, bread, and cheese again and drove on at 9 o'clock, now for the first time in the Chevrolet. As there were only 3 seats, the chauffeur had to sit on the mudguard and the manager drove a short distance together. Luckily it was only 4 km to Bazirgan, and the road was up above. So we arrived in good weather at the two massive buildings at the outermost northwest tip of Iran, in which the police and customs are housed. Then there were a few sheds for goods and a few...

Encouraged by the good example, the assistant brought another 7 Turkish Lira, for which I gave him 14 Toman. Then I only had a little over 10 Toman left, which Strauss had for me. Then we enjoyed the magnificent view of Mount Ararat, which according to the director's statements must have been very beautiful yesterday. We, however, tried in vain to penetrate with our gazes to the ruins of Noah's Ark, because the height of the mountain was wrapped in a black, impenetrable fog. We then said goodbye to the director with mutual good wishes and went down into the ruins of the already mentioned Turkish airplane that wait there for pickup by the Turks. After that, we went with Abdil, the manager, into the village, where I sent the telegram to you, beloved parents, and then Abdil invited us to lunch in his hut, which consisted of mud and contained 2 rooms. He lives there during his business in Bazirgan and wanted to stay there a while longer now. The only inventory formed a samovar, a *silou* [carpet/mat], and a few cushions. But the lunch was excellent, consisting for the last time of real Iranian chicken, fried potatoes, a towel, and grapes, which we consumed sitting on the *silou*. There I also exchanged the rest of the money.

Then we drove further between a few hills through the 4 km distant Turkish border village of Gürbulak. Nobody spoke Persian there, let alone any other language familiar to us. Communication was therefore made through the mediation of Ismael, our chauffeur. We left our money, silver, and collection in the passes entered, because there were no seals there. They took 2 new tires off the driver and searched the car down to the smallest detail. Meanwhile, we strolled around on the hills and painted unseemly words in the fresh snow. At darkness it went further to a larger place, about 20 km from the border, Bayazit. There we found an eatery with low tables and menus; but we went into the kitchen and pointed at the pots, whose contents numbering about 20 different dishes stood on the hearth. This car was a Ford that had no side windows anymore and was only sketchily covered with a tarpaulin. In it we now drove as 5 persons in the late afternoon. There were 2 trucks, the second a Chevrolet on which Strauss's large luggage was. We wanted to reach Karagossa, barely 100 km back, and reach it. In the thick snow there was no road to be seen. Only 2 snowed-over ruts showed that cars had driven along here some time ago. It was horribly cold as we drove off. The heavy machines moved forward only step-by-step in first gear; the first 20 km went fine; for we needed only 2 hours for that. Then our Ford, driving ahead, got stuck. It was an unpleasant situation in the freezing, clear, night-time mountains, deep snow as far as the eye could see. The chauffeur pulled all his strength out of the engine and the car body shivered under the strain, as if it wanted to burst any moment. It helped nothing. Two men had to shovel the car free. Only then did we move a few hundred meters further, although the incline was small. Always and again it had to be shoveled. Sometimes it looked hopeless; and then we had to get out to help push. Already upon stepping out of the car one sank up to above the knee in the snow. The dreadfully cold air sliced the skin. Around the ears I had laid my woolen scarf. After a few seconds I could no longer breathe through my nose, as it was frozen shut. On the entire drive we had not yet met a house, a human being. Far and wide only mountains and vast amounts of snow. My feet were frozen stiff, despite having put on the mountain boots over thick colored socks on Strauss's advice. Over the gloves I had another pair of stockings pulled; but all that could not keep off the appalling cold. I cursed the whole trip, America, and in particular human powerlessness against nature's forces, and I would have gladly given a kingdom for a warm bed. But nobody took this unique opportunity, to become king of a mountain height, while the others shoveled on and we stomped in the snow, looking through the improbably clear night in moonlight at Ararat, a sight that would have delighted us on a summer night. Then it went a few km downhill, everything in first gear with full engine power; but it went at least until we got stuck again at the next high point. One of our drivers came running back after a few minutes like a crying child and then collapsed. Then we prepared ourselves for spending the night in the open car, crammed together as five, a pleasant prospect, especially since hunger plagued us and we had eaten nothing for 24 hours. **Türkali, 3. January 1940.**

It falls hard for me to overcome myself to write. How immensely depressing this decade starts for us. We sit here in a dark hole, a few stacked stones stuck together with mud; the only inventory are an iron stove, a kerosene lamp, and 2 woven mats. The twilight is produced by a window in the ceiling, 20 cm square, which is drifted over with snow. And the whole thing is located in high mountains in an unimaginable mountain village. We had taken a desperate attempt to overcome the mountains and stuck short before the pass in meter-high snow in a horrific snowstorm. After hours of exertion we left sleds and luggage up there and saved ourselves and the horses back into this dump. But I will tell in order how it came to this!

The awakening in that lonely house took place on Dec 25th of last year. It was a wonderful sunny winter day, and as we stepped out in front of the house into the warm sun, before us the light-flooded snow landscape, we were reconciled with our fate. Then we also saw a friendly village in the valley a short distance away. We stomped without coats in the warm sun through the high snow; soon however my nose froze up. Therefore I went into the first house to warm up. We were received very friendly and recognized by the lists lying around that we were at the village elder's. Communication was impossible. Nevertheless we conversed for a while remarkably with our hands, while we were served tea. Then we walked further past a small facility with the war memorial and the school into an inn. There sat already our chauffeurs and we learned now that the village was called **Taslicay**, that in the night 35 degrees of cold had been, and we would now have to wait until gasoline from the 35km distant Karagossa arrived. The whole village ran together to marvel at us, including one who spoke some Persian. He undertook the communication subsequently. We then went back once more to fetch our coats, the writing pad, and playing cards, and then stayed into the night in the inn. First we learned the Turkish numbers; then I laid out a list of the most important words that we constantly needed, and finally I began this report. You, dear Papa, will probably search in vain with your finger on the map. When you go up from Baghdad with the Euphrates, and where it splits, follow the southern source stream, then you finally come to Karagossa (Karagöse) and right at the beginning, where the Euphrates is as wide as the Klia [river], lies Taslicay.

Most of the time of the day we spent playing cards. In between we joined in with the lunch that they left for us, as inedible. How good was the Iranian rice against this surrogate they called rice. Good rice is very expensive in Turkey, and now we understood why our chauffeur had smiled satisfied at the border for 25 pounds of rice 5 Lira duty...

**7)** We bought our supper together: hazelnuts, raisins, apples, cheese, bread, and butter. The program of the next day did not differ significantly from the previous day's, except that we did without lunch and Strauss mortally offended me by saying that I had a red beard. Otherwise we cursed and swore at this miserable delay; for back then we did not yet know that our health, perhaps even our lives, were saved by it. For without delays we would have landed with astonishing precision right in the middle of the earthquake.

In the course of the afternoon the gasoline came, and the next morning we were to continue. We audibly breathed a sigh of relief when, on 27 December, we sat in our cold Ford and drove off. The Chevrolet, which had got stuck 300m ahead of us, was already gone. The visibility in the hazy weather was miserable. One could barely make out the drifted-over ruts. So it happened that, at the spot where the Chevrolet had stood, we got stuck in its tracks. During the monstrous exertions of the engine to get free, a rear axle broke, and with that it was all over. We stomped disgruntled back into the village and, through the mediation of the Persian speaker, rented a sled. An hour later we loaded our luggage from the Ford onto the sled, except for Strauss's two large pieces, which were on the Chevrolet. Then we set off. I had wrapped my woolen shawl picturesquely around my head and ears. Strauss had his ski cap, which was invaluable, and 2 left gloves; the right ones he had lost on the way. Otherwise it was as uncomfortable as could be. My feet soon froze in my mountain boots, so that I could no longer move them. After a few kilometers the fat chauffeur of the Chevrolet came toward us; we stopped and he spoke excitedly with the driver. He gestured wildly at us with both hands. As we drove on, we saw the mess. The car had gone a little off the road and overturned. The many sacks of goods lay peacefully scattered about the area together with Sträusschen's suitcases. We loaded the two suitcases onto the sled and drove on. At nightfall we arrived in Karagossa, a larger village with electric lighting, according to the map the capital of the province of Agri. In the hotel Strauss pulled the shoes off my feet, which were completely frozen stiff. With great pain I thawed them out, while Strauss inspected our room and came back satisfied. Then we heard that the connection to Erzurum was impossible, and went to the nearest kitchen to dine lavishly. When we returned to the hotel, we found barely a place. Only a few of those present were consuming anything. Most were playing "Puff" [backgammon], apparently the local national game; for everyone can play it, and even children play it with ape-like speed. We soon went to our room, where it was shockingly cold. Even when we lit the wooden logs in the stove, it became only temporarily and insignificantly warmer. The beds were clean, and so for the first time in a long while I undressed; but I could not get warm, and so I spent the night getting dressed again. I put on stockings and every sweater I had, put on Sträusschen's ski cap and tied my shawl over it; then I also laid our two winter coats over me. When I had finally fallen asleep toward morning, I was soon woken by Sträusschen's scornful laughter at the sight of me, which, however, quickly froze again at the sight of the water carafe, in which the water was frozen to the bottom. Our morning toilette was in keeping with the cold, and half-frozen we reached the guest room. We were rid of our 130 Toman, sat in Karagossa instead of Trebizond, and knew that we had to rely on ourselves alone. An unpleasant situation. Our day was filled with inquiries at the bus office about when the road would be clear, sounding out every chauffeur, playing cards and backgammon, gorging, and in between I wrote my report. The innkeepers of the hotel were charming. They were the only ones with whom we could communicate, for they spoke Persian, and they helped us in a touching way to get further. But the road was and remained closed to cars for an indefinite time, and there was no other way out than to decide on the 185km journey by sled.

The horses did not look particularly trustworthy, but made an honest effort. Their master had to beat them constantly to keep them at a trot. Nevertheless they often had long opportunities to go at a walk; for every time we met someone on the way, the driver jumped down and held an extended little chat. Then he came running after us. And unfortunately we met many people. So we had ample opportunity to enjoy the bracing air and watch the apple growth, which provided varied topics of conversation for a while. After 35 km the day's goal was reached, in a village named Alaskirt. Our luggage was unloaded and we ate roasted legs of mutton, the only edible thing in the local kitchen. It was only 4 o'clock, but pitch dark. In return it becomes light at 6 o'clock in the morning. We sat in the village inn and had to put up with the usual staring, for which our driver gave the necessary explanations. Then we drank tea and thought of earlier New Year's Eves. My thoughts were with the loved ones in Shanghai; but I had only tea, which I could drink to Aunt Lottchen's health. We went to bed early. I often woke up from a scratching sound that seemed to come from a cat. At 5 o'clock someone stomped into the chamber to wake us, and then a very feeble "Prosit Neujahr, Fritz" [Happy New Year, Fritz] woke me completely. Strauss, the poor fellow, had barely slept; for the scratching sound came from water that had been dripping constantly through the ceiling onto his bed. The new decade continued to start quite unpleasantly. At the first dawn we went on in a fine drizzling rain. There were 3 sleds. 2 had joined us the evening before, with a young Turk, his wife, 4 children, and luggage. Now we went into the dreaded mountains, constantly uphill, and we moved forward only at a walk. Luckily the rain soon stopped. Around 10 o'clock we had covered 22 km and stopped in front of the gendarmerie in Türkali, before us the high mountains which we had to cross. The driver telephoned to Tahir, the day's goal, which is 18 km from here, and got word that the road was clear. So we drove on. After a few hundred meters every trace was lost in the deep snow and we had to get out, because the horses could not pull the heavy sleds through. One man went ahead to find the path. If he sank into the snow only up to his knees, then we were on the right path. A violent storm whipped sharp ice crystals into our faces as we stomped uphill. From the exertion we sweated all over our bodies despite the gruesome cold. The higher we came, the sharper the storm became, the snow ever deeper. So for hours we fought our way forward step by step. When, shortly before the ridge, we came around a bend in the path while I was stomping ahead, I suddenly lay in the snow. The dreadful storm had blown me over. The horses were stuck in the snow up to above their knees. It was the end. We could not see 5m ahead. With a gruesome howling the storm whipped the snow against us. Added to this came the crying of the children from the covered sled. The drivers and we two were covered from top to bottom with a crust of ice. One of the drivers and Strauss tried to find a way and vanished in the snowstorm, while we tried to get the horses free. Meanwhile the driver came back. Strauss remained missing. Over an hour had passed and I was extremely worried about him. They began to unharness the horses. Finally Strauss came back, completely out of breath. He had sunk in up to his neck, had lost a rubber overshoe in the process, and had to crawl back on his belly. Moving forward was out of the question. In the attempt to lead the horses back past the sleds, one fell and rolled 3m down the slope. When they tried to pull it up, it rolled ever deeper.

**9)** The situation was desperate. The father of the family had meanwhile crawled out from under the cover and drew a revolver, with which he fired twice into the air. The bang died away in the storm. For the fallen horse a path was dug to the side, along which they managed to get it back up. Then with our last strength the sled with the children was turned around and we went back, after we had been up there in this hell for over 2 hours. The two sleds with the luggage remained up there. Our track from the way up had long been blown over. The 6 km to Türkali stretched endlessly. At times we rode on the horses, but after a short time froze stiff with cold for lack of active movement. The first house we reached was the gendarmerie. We staggered in, had our ice crusts scraped off, and undressed completely. I poured the water out of my shoes; my trousers and socks I had to wring out. In many places the skin was inflamed from the wetness, and drying off hurt badly. The gendarmes were very helpful, made coffee and tea, and brought bread. We stayed there the whole afternoon; then we landed in the hole in which we have now already spent 3 nights. Since the day before yesterday we have not set a foot outside the door, because a heavy snowstorm did not allow it. The primitiveness is indescribable. The huts in Pascalé are palaces compared to it. No cigarettes, no tea. There is only water to drink, from a tin bowl that is passed around the circle. There I explained to a German-speaking railway official that we had no money left and hadn't been allowed to call our uncle, who was supposed to sort it out. It was very satisfying for us to watch the policeman get chewed out, and we were allowed to place our suitcases in the hallway. We had our own compartment on the Orient Express along with a policeman. As we departed, a large colorful poster laughed down at us reading: "Istanbul welcomes you". We had a rather different definition of a welcome and were glad to get away from this hospitable city. In the morning, the policeman left us, and at the next station we were in Pythion, Greece, where we had to transfer. There we fell into another state of shock, because the connecting train to Athens was gone—our express train was delayed, and the next train was not scheduled to leave for another 24 hours. It was now once again impossible to reach the Citta di Bari and, with it, the Conte di Savoia. We endured the customs inspection, had to have the large trunks and the stove lead-sealed and checked, which cost a lot of money, and waited out the evening in that tiny hamlet. We spent the night in the waiting room and, very early the next morning, took a diesel railcar further through all of Thrace along the flooded plain of a wide river, where there were many cranes and other fowl. We rode through Philistraton, Syndetikon, Shaneion, and whatever else they were called, to Alexandroupolis, where we had to transfer again. This time we had a connection and got two window seats in a padded train compartment. Even though the weather was gloomy, the ride through Macedonia was beautiful. Here, too, were many rocky mountains; but they were not as barren as those in Iran and Turkey, but covered in green with many small trees, framing the snow-capped peaks in the background nicely. Unfortunately, there was as little tobacco to be seen now as there was of the eternally radiant sun for which this landscape is world-famous. At 10 o'clock at night we arrived in Salonika, where our compartment became full, so we had to spend a very cramped night. Very early in the morning we looked out the window again, enjoyed the charming landscape, and made wise comparisons regarding the historical background. Shortly past Thebes, we descended into the valley towards Athens, with the port of Piraeus and the truly blue Mediterranean Sea way in the background. We rode straight on to Piraeus to inquire about the next ship. At 11:30 we arrived and...
