# The Reality of the 1939–1940 Route Stops

While major hubs like Tehran, Tabriz, Erzurum, Istanbul, Athens, Naples, and New York City remain major world cities today, many of the smaller stops in eastern Anatolia—particularly around the high-altitude mountain passes—were never large cities:

* **Renamed Cities & Historic Ruins:** 
  * **Bayazit $\rightarrow$ Doğubayazıt:** Old Bayazit was built around a castle at the base of a mountain canyon. After sustaining heavy damage in the early 20th century, the entire town relocated down to the plain in the 1930s and was renamed *Doğubayazıt* ("East Bayazit").
  * **Karaköse $\rightarrow$ Ağrı:** The provincial capital was called *Karaköse* in 1939 (the letter spells it "Karagossa"/"Karagöse"), but was later officially renamed *Ağrı* (after nearby Mt. Ararat, *Ağrı Dağı*; rename year to verify).

* **Mountain Pass "Huts" & Sled Stations:** 
  * **Türkali, Delal & Kizildice:** These were essentially tiny mountain hamlets, roadside post stations, or clusters of huts flanking the dangerous **Tahir Pass** (*Tahir Geçidi*, 2,496 m elevation). During brutal eastern Anatolian winters, travelers switched from stuck motor vehicles to horse-drawn sleds or waited out blizzards here. Today, many have either shrunk into tiny farming hamlets (*köy*) or disappeared from modern highway maps as paved roads replaced dirt tracks.

* **Highway & Railway Junctions:** 
  * **Çetinkaya:** Not a major city, but a vital railway junction where southern and western train lines split. It remains a small town in Sivas Province today, recognizable mainly by its station.
  * **Pythion:** A small border village on the Evros River between Turkey and Greece, famous at the time primarily as a border check stop on the line of the *Orient Express*.

Many of these 1939/1940 stops were indeed glorified winter shelters, border checkpoints, or tiny mountain relay stations!

# Misc

1. The Erzincan Earthquake
This is the **1939 Erzincan earthquake**. It struck eastern Turkey shortly before 2 AM local time on December 27, 1939 (late on December 26 UTC). Its magnitude was 7.8, and it was Turkey's deadliest earthquake of the 20th century, killing about 32,000–33,000 people across the region and almost entirely destroying the city. Heavy snow and cold hampered relief. Fritz passed through on about January 12, 1940, roughly two weeks later. His figure of over 13,000 dead out of 18,000 inhabitants (Letter p. 14) is an eyewitness estimate for the city itself, not the regional total. The −28°C reading is from Sivas, not Erzincan. The tent shelters for the injured, and the freight trains used as field hospitals at Sivas (also p. 14), are consistent with historical accounts. Fritz also notes (p. 7) that their snow delays kept them out of the region when the quake hit.

2. "Spoons" vs. Dental Tools
It is not a mistranslation, but rather a specific dental instrument. In German, dental impression trays are called Abdrucklöffel (literally "impression spoons"). In operative dentistry, small scoop-like hand tools are also called Exkavatorlöffel ("excavator spoons"). This happened in Erzurum (Letter p. 12). Strauss brought out a few impression trays to show hotel guests that they had no real instruments. One guest bargained for 4 of them and paid 4.50 Lira, which the penniless travelers badly needed. Fritz wondered whether the buyer meant to give them to his wife as cookie molds. The exact German word is to be confirmed against the transcription in `letter/de/page-12.txt`.

3. "Detective with a Shape"
This is a literal translation of an idiomatic/descriptive German phrasing. It appears in Letter p. 13 (Erzurum): a detective came in "with a shape" and asked them to come to the police. The German wording has not yet been checked. Confirm it in `letter/de/page-13.txt` before interpreting it. A plausible reading is *Gestalt* in the sense of "a figure/person" (another man with him) or "an imposing figure".

4. The Card Game "66"
Sechsundsechzig (Sixty-six) is a famous, traditional 6-card trick-and-draw card game popular across Central Europe and Germany (often played with a 24-card deck). The goal is to be the first player to score 66 card points through tricks and declarations. It was a very common casual two-player game for travelers to pass the time. See https://bicyclecards.com/how-to-play/sixty-six/

In the letter, Fritz and Strauss play 66 while snowbound at Kızıldize (p. 5), play cards for days at Taşlıçay and Karaköse (pp. 6–7), and play 66 and poker with Turkish officers on the train past Erzincan (p. 14). The letter does *not* mention 66 at Türkali; there they had a gramophone, a kid goat's head, and vermin. Later, on the *Conte di Savoia*, they play **Skat** with Hungarian passengers (p. 20).

5. The Book: "Sandlungen der Liebe"
Fritz reads this on the night train from Eskişehir to Istanbul (Letter p. 15). "Sandlungen" is almost certainly an OCR/translation error for ***Wandlungen der Liebe*** ("Transformations of Love"). Confirm the spelling on the scan (`letter/de/page-15.txt`). Which book this is has not been verified, so don't name an author on the site until it is.

6. What is "Città di Bari"?
It is a ship, not a place. Mr. Goldenberg booked them on it from Istanbul to Naples to meet the *Conte di Savoia* (Letter pp. 15–17). Police proceedings made them miss it on Jan 19, and the missed connection at Pythion ended the plan to catch it at Piraeus. Background (verify through media research before publishing): an Italian passenger ship on the Adriatica/Eastern Mediterranean service between Istanbul, Piraeus, and Italian ports. Build year and tonnage are unverified.

7. Records of the "Egitto"
The letter calls it a 3,500-ton "luxury steamer" of the same shipping company as the *Città di Bari*, sailing Piraeus → Corinth Canal → Brindisi on Jan 22–23, 1940 (Letter p. 20). Operator, build year and history are unverified; see `media/chapter-05.md` for sourced details.

8. The "Hand-Towel" Food
The "hand towels" (*Handtücher*) are Iranian flatbreads, most likely thin *lavash* (possibly *sangak*). These are baked in wide, flexible sheets and folded like cloth. The episodes are in Chapter 2, not Chapter 1: sheets "of the best quality" with sheep's cheese at a courtyard stop on the Tabriz → Khoy night bus (Letter pp. 2–3), and "hand-towels of lesser quality" at a dive bar about 10 km before Maku. There they tore off a strip, topped it with cheese, fat, or a flour-sugar-mutton-fat porridge, and "rolled it up like a giant cigarette" (Letter p. 3).
