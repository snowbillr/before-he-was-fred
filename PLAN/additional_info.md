# The Reality of the 1939–1940 Route Stops

While major hubs like Tehran, Tabriz, Erzurum, Istanbul, Athens, Naples, and New York City remain major world cities today, many of the smaller stops in eastern Anatolia—particularly around the high-altitude mountain passes—were never large cities:

* **Renamed Cities & Historic Ruins:** 
  * **Bayazit $\rightarrow$ Doğubayazıt:** Old Bayazit was built around a castle at the base of a mountain canyon. After sustaining heavy damage in the early 20th century, the entire town relocated down to the plain in the 1930s and was renamed *Doğubayazıt* ("East Bayazit").
  * **Karaköse $\rightarrow$ Ağrı:** The provincial capital was called *Karaköse* in 1939, but was later officially renamed *Ağrı* (after nearby Mt. Ararat).

* **Mountain Pass "Huts" & Sled Stations:** 
  * **Türkali, Delal & Kizildice:** These were essentially tiny mountain hamlets, roadside post stations, or clusters of huts flanking the dangerous **Tahir Pass** (*Tahir Geçidi*, 2,496 m elevation). During brutal eastern Anatolian winters, travelers switched from stuck motor vehicles to horse-drawn sleds or waited out blizzards here. Today, many have either shrunk into tiny farming hamlets (*köy*) or disappeared from modern highway maps as paved roads replaced dirt tracks.

* **Highway & Railway Junctions:** 
  * **Çetinkaya:** Not a major city, but a vital railway junction where southern and western train lines split. It remains a small town in Sivas Province today, recognizable mainly by its station.
  * **Pythion:** A small border village on the Evros River between Turkey and Greece, famous at the time primarily as a border check stop on the line of the *Orient Express*.

Many of these 1939/1940 stops were indeed glorified winter shelters, border checkpoints, or tiny mountain relay stations!

# Misc

1. The Erzincan Earthquake
Yes, this was the infamous 13th Erzincan Earthquake, which struck eastern Turkey on December 26–27, 1939. It was a massive magnitude 7.8 earthquake, one of the worst natural disasters in modern Turkish history. It killed over 32,000 to 33,000 people and destroyed the city almost entirely. The writer’s description on Page 4 of 13,000 dead out of an 18,000 population, the minus 28°C weather, and the temporary tent field hospitals matches historical accounts remarkably well.

2. "Spoons" vs. Dental Tools
It is not a mistranslation, but rather a specific dental instrument. In German, dental impression trays are called Abdrucklöffel (literally "impression spoons"). In operative dentistry, small scoop-like hand tools are also called Exkavatorlöffel ("excavator spoons"). The writer explicitly notes the absurdity of the situation: a local bought 4 impression trays (Abdrucklöffel) for 4.50 Lira, and the narrator cheekily wondered if the buyer intended to use them as cookie molds (Plätzchenformen).

3. "Detective with a Shape"
This is a literal translation of an idiomatic/descriptive German phrasing. In German, the text reads "...kam ein Detektiv mit einer Gestalt rein", which translates to "a detective came in with a [certain/particular] figure/person" or "a detective with an imposing presence." Here, Gestalt simply means a person, figure, or stature accompanying him, rather than a physical shape object.

4. The Card Game "66"
Sechsundsechzig (Sixty-six) is a famous, traditional 6-card trick-and-draw card game popular across Central Europe and Germany (often played with a 24-card deck). The goal is to be the first player to score 66 card points through tricks and declarations. It was a very common casual two-player game for travelers to pass the time. See https://bicyclecards.com/how-to-play/sixty-six/

5. The Book: "Sandlungen der Liebe"
This is a small typo in the original typescript or a misreading of faded text. It refers to Wandlungen der Liebe ("Transmutations/Transformations of Love"), a popular German novel or romantic literary work from that era.

6. What is "Città di Bari"?
It is a ship, not a place. The Città di Bari was an Italian passenger cargo motor vessel (built in 1928) operated by the Adriatica line that ran scheduled passenger routes across the Mediterranean, connecting Istanbul, Piraeus (Athens), Brindisi, and Naples.

7. Records of the "Egitto"
Yes! The SS Egitto was a real passenger steamer operated by the Italian shipping line S.A. Navigatione Aegyptos (and later integrated into Adriatica or Lloyd Triestino routes). Built in the 1920s, it operated the passage between Greece (Piraeus) and Italy (Brindisi/Naples).

8. The "Hand-Towel" Food
The references to "hand towels" (Handtuch in German) are Iranian flatbreads—specifically Lavash or Sangak. These thin, flexible breads are baked in wide sheets, rolled up or folded like fabric, and often wrapped in actual cloth towels to prevent them from drying out. The travelers ate meat, fat, or cheese by wrapping it inside torn strips of flatbread like a roll.
