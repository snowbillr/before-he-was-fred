### Context cards

**Card — "A deportation order to leave the country that very same day" (Letter p. 16–17)**
By January 1940 the two travellers had overstayed Turkey's foreigner-registration and transit limits (Letter p. 16 mentions a 24-hour registration rule and a 14-day stay limit) because of the Tahir Pass blizzard and the Erzincan earthquake. They were tried in a Galata police court and sentenced to 14 days in prison and a 25-lira fine, which was "remitted… because it was the first time". They then got their exit visas together with an order to leave Turkey that same day. The Simplon-Orient-Express was the obvious way out: it was the overnight international train from Istanbul to Western Europe, with a through connection to Athens. *Sources: Letter p. 16–17; [Orient Express — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orient_Express).* (I could not find a verified primary source for the 1939–40 Turkish registration rules themselves, so the card should credit them to the letter.)

**Card — The *Città di Bari* and her sisters (Letter p. 16–17, 20)**
The *Città di Bari* was not a city. She was a 3,220–3,339 GRT Italian passenger motor ship built in 1928 at the Stabilimento Tecnico Triestino, together with three identical sisters: *Rodi*, *Egeo* and *Egitto*. She was built for the Puglia line of Bari, which merged into the Venice-based Adriatica company in 1932, and she sailed Adriatica's line 54 linking the Tyrrhenian ports with Piraeus and Istanbul. She carried 89 cabin passengers at about 14.5 knots. Five months after Fritz missed her, on 15 June 1940, the Regia Marina requisitioned her as an auxiliary cruiser, and her sisters met the same fate. *Source: [Città di Bari — Wikipedia (it)](https://it.wikipedia.org/wiki/Citt%C3%A0_di_Bari); [Adriatica di Navigazione — Wikipedia (it)](https://it.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adriatica_di_Navigazione).*

### Context card

**Card — Pythion, a border station built for a vanished empire (Letter p. 17)**
The Chemins de fer Orientaux reached Pythion (Ottoman *Kuleliburgaz*) in 1873 on the Istanbul–Edirne–Vienna line, and in 1874 opened a 112 km branch south to Dedeağaç (today's Alexandroupolis). The 1923 Treaty of Lausanne put the Greek–Turkish border on the Evros River just east of the village. As a result the Istanbul–Europe main line, Orient Express included, entered Greece at Pythion, went back into Turkey at Edirne-Karaağaç, and then crossed into Bulgaria. This zig-zag lasted until 1971. Travellers to Athens changed here for the line to Alexandroupolis and Thessaloniki. When the delayed express arrived, the Athens connection had already left, so Fritz and Strauss were stuck for 24 hours, paying to have their trunks and their stove lead-sealed. *Source: [Pythio — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pythio); Letter p. 17.*

### Context card

**Card — "Philistraton, Syndetikon, Shaneion…" (Letter p. 17)**
The station names Fritz half-remembered do not match any stations I could verify on the Pythion–Alexandroupolis line. Either the typescript garbled them or he was joking about how alike the Greek names sounded. The site should show them in his own spelling with an editor's note rather than trying to identify them. The "wide river" with its "flooded plain" is the Evros (Bulgarian *Maritsa*, Turkish *Meriç*), whose lower course has formed the Greek–Turkish border since 1923. The line from Pythion to Alexandroupolis runs down its west bank toward the delta, which today is a protected wetland known for its waterbirds. *Source: Letter p. 17; [Maritsa — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maritsa).*

## 5. Alexandroupolis stop & leg to Salonika through Macedonia (Jan 20, 1940)

*Letter p. 17: transferred at Alexandroupolis, "two window seats in a padded train compartment"; Macedonian hills "covered in green with many small trees… snow-capped peaks"; "as little tobacco to be seen now as there was of the eternally radiant sun".*

- Alexandroupolis media: see the 1893 Dedeağaç station photo above. **No free 1930s photo of Alexandroupolis was found.**

### Context cards

**Card — From Dedeağaç to Alexandroupolis (Letter p. 17)**
Alexandroupolis began in the 19th century as the Ottoman fishing village of Dedeağaç and grew because of the railway. Bulgaria took it in 1912 and Greece in the Second Balkan War, the Treaty of Bucharest gave it back to Bulgaria, and Greece took it again after World War I. In 1920 it was renamed for King Alexander of Greece, and the Treaty of Lausanne (1923) confirmed it as Greek. In 1940 it was the junction where the Pythion branch met the Thessaloniki–Constantinople line. *Source: [Alexandroupolis — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexandroupolis).*

**Card — The Salonica–Constantinople Junction railway (Letter p. 17)**
The roughly 440 km line from Alexandroupolis west to Thessaloniki, by way of Xanthi, Drama and Serres, was built in 1896 by the French-owned *Compagnie du Chemin de Fer Ottoman Jonction Salonique–Constantinople*. Fritz's remark about the missing tobacco makes sense here: the Xanthi–Drama–Kavala belt along this line was the heart of Greece's "Oriental" tobacco country. In January, though, the fields would have been bare. *Source: [Thessaloniki–Alexandroupolis railway — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thessaloniki%E2%80%93Alexandroupolis_railway).* (The tobacco claim is general knowledge; add a dedicated source before publishing, e.g. a Kavala tobacco-warehouse museum page.)

### Context card

**Card — The new Athens–Salonika main line (Letter p. 17)**
The overnight train ran on the standard-gauge Piraeus–Thessaloniki main line, which passes Larissa and Thebes before dropping into the Attic plain. Today it is the Piraeus–Platy railway. Fritz's comment about the "historical background" probably refers to Thebes, the city of Oedipus and of the Theban hegemony of the 4th century BC. Trains on this line ran right through Athens to the harbour station at Piraeus, which is how Fritz could "ride straight on to Piraeus". *Source: [Piraeus–Platy railway — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Piraeus%E2%80%93Platy_railway); Letter p. 17.*

### Context card

**Card — The "luxury steamer" *Egitto* (Letter p. 20)**
The *Egitto* was one of the four 1928 Trieste-built sisters described in Section 1. She was a 3,200–3,300 GRT **motor ship** (FIAT diesel, one propeller, about 14.5 knots), not a steamer, so Fritz's "3,500-ton luxury steamer" is close on size but wrong on propulsion. She belonged to the same Adriatica company as the *Città di Bari*. That is why Strauss could simply "exchange our tickets without difficulty". In June 1940 she too was requisitioned as an auxiliary cruiser. *Source: [Città di Bari — Wikipedia (it)](https://it.wikipedia.org/wiki/Citt%C3%A0_di_Bari) (covers the whole class).*

### Context card

**Card — Cutting the Isthmus (Letter p. 20)**
Rulers had dreamed of a canal across the Isthmus of Corinth since Periander in the 7th century BC. Nero actually broke ground in AD 67, using Jewish prisoners from the First Jewish–Roman War, but the project stopped after his death. Modern work began in 1881, bankrupted its first builders, and the canal finally opened in 1893. It is a sea-level cut with no locks, **6.4 km long** and only **24.6 m wide** at the waterline, with walls of rock up to about 80 m high. Because of its narrowness and repeated landslides it never attracted the traffic its promoters hoped for. In 1940, though, it still saved ships like the *Egitto* the long trip around the Peloponnese. *Source: [Corinth Canal — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corinth_Canal).*

### Context card

**Card — Brindisi, Italy's gateway to the East (Letter p. 20)**
Brindisi sits at the heel of Italy where the Roman Via Appia ended (its terminal column still stands on the waterfront). For centuries it was the main Italian port for ships to Greece and the Levant, and in the 1930s Adriatica ships from Piraeus and the eastern Mediterranean called there (the *Città di Bari* also ran a Brindisi–Piraeus–Rhodes–Alexandria route). In January 1940 Italy was at peace: Mussolini had declared Italy "non-belligerent" when war broke out in September 1939 and did not join the war until 10 June 1940. That is why German-Jewish refugees could still cross Italy to catch an Italian liner to New York. *Sources: [Città di Bari — Wikipedia (it)](https://it.wikipedia.org/wiki/Citt%C3%A0_di_Bari); [Conte di Savoia — italianliners.com](https://www.italianliners.com/conte-di-savoia-en) (Italy's entry into the war on 10 June 1940).*

### Context card

**Card — "Germany was not accepting calls from abroad" (Letter p. 20)**
Fritz had looked forward the whole journey to phoning his family in Germany from Naples, the last stop before America. Since Britain and France declared war in September 1939, telephone traffic between Germany and many foreign countries had been cut back or monitored. The letter is our only source for the specific refusal he got in Naples. Italy was still at peace, so letters and some telegrams were still getting through. *Source: Letter p. 20.* (I found no free source to verify the exact state of Italy–Germany telephone service in January 1940. Present this as Fritz's experience, not a general rule.)

## Flags — possible errors in plan docs / translation

1. **`additional_info.md` #7 (the *Egitto*) is wrong.** She was not operated by "S.A. Navigatione Aegyptos", was not an "SS" (steamship) and was not Lloyd Triestino. She was an **Adriatica motor ship (MV/M/N)** built in 1928 at Stabilimento Tecnico Triestino as a sister of the *Città di Bari*. ([it.wikipedia](https://it.wikipedia.org/wiki/Citt%C3%A0_di_Bari))
2. **`additional_info.md` #6 (*Città di Bari*)** is mostly right but should say: built 1928 for the *Puglia* company of Bari, part of Adriatica from 1932, motor ship of about 3,220–3,339 GRT. It did not run "Istanbul–Piraeus–Brindisi–Naples" as one stated route. Its documented line was no. 54 (Tyrrhenian ports–Piraeus–Istanbul), which fits the letter's "set sail for Naples".
3. **design_doc.md Ch. 5 and plan pages call the ship "*SS Egitto*"**. Should be *MV Egitto* or just *Egitto*.
4. **Letter p. 20 says the Corinth Canal is "approximately 3 km long".** It is 6.4 km. Keep the letter's words but add an editor's note.
5. **Letter p. 20: "a Cordell marvel of engineering"** is almost certainly a transcription/translation error (perhaps German *kolossal* or a misread word). Check against the German original.
6. **Date ambiguity, Piraeus → Brindisi.** The letter puts the Piraeus departure at Jan 21 (the 24-hour wait at Pythion on Jan 19–20, then Salonika at 10 pm and Piraeus at 11:30 the next morning), then says "the next day, on January 23" they reached Brindisi. One of these is off by a day: either the sailing was Jan 22 or the crossing took two nights. `route.md` (Piraeus Jan 21, Brindisi Jan 23) follows the letter as written. Flag it in the timetable widget.
7. **design_doc.md Ch. 5 dates "Jan 17–24"**: the Istanbul arrival was Jan 16 (route.md), and the train left Istanbul on the night of ~Jan 18. Align with the new chapter dates (Jan 16–24).
8. **Letter p. 17 says the *Città di Bari* "would dock in Piraeus two days later"**, which is consistent with her Istanbul–Piraeus route. No issue, just confirming.
