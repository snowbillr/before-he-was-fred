## Factual corrections & uncertainties

1. **Earthquake figures.** `additional_info.md` is right on the big picture (Mw 7.8, ~33,000 dead). Use the authoritative figure **32,968 dead, ~100,000 injured, 116,720 buildings seriously damaged, intensity XII, at 01:57 local time on Dec. 27, 1939**, along a ~360 km rupture of the North Anatolian Fault ([1939 Erzincan earthquake](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1939_Erzincan_earthquake)). The letter's "13,000 of 18,000 inhabitants" of Erzincan town (Letter p. 14) is what Fritz was told on the spot — present it as his report, not as a verified figure.
2. **Commons dates for the *Vakit* PDFs are wrong.** Files named "Vakit 1939 birincikanun NN" are labelled "1939 Ocak (January) NN" on Commons, but *birincikânun* is the old Turkish name for **December**; the Dec. 28 issue's front page carries the Erzincan headline, which confirms December 1939. Caption with the corrected date.
3. **Dates in the letter.** On p. 14 a "15.1.40" heading appears in the middle of the Jan. 12 landslide passage — probably the date Fritz wrote that section, not the date of the events. Reconstructed sequence: Hasankale night of Jan. 7; Erzurum arrival Jan. 8 (Letter p. 11–12); train finally leaves Erzurum Jan. 11 at 9:00 (Letter p. 13); landslide transfer and Erzincan on Jan. 12; Çetinkaya at 17:00 and Sivas at 21:00 the next day (Letter p. 14); Ankara at noon, Eskişehir 23:00 (Letter p. 15); Haydarpaşa ~07:00 Jan. 16. `route.md` broadly matches this — but cross-check Erzincan (Jan. 12) vs. Sivas.
4. **"Ferry to Pera on the Golden Horn"** (Letter p. 15): the Haydarpaşa ferries docked at Karaköy (Galata), at the mouth of the Golden Horn by the Galata Bridge; Pera (Beyoğlu) is the hill district above. Fine as a paraphrase; captions should say Karaköy/Galata.
5. **Delal** location is unconfirmed (see chapter 3 file).

### Context card — a railway left by war
The narrow-gauge line through Horasan was a legacy of the Russian occupation of eastern Anatolia during WWI, connecting Erzurum toward Sarıkamış and Kars, the old Russian frontier. In January 1940 it still did not connect with the new standard-gauge main line that had reached Erzurum only months earlier (Letter p. 11–12). Source: [Kars–Erzurum railway context, Erzurum railway station (tr)](https://tr.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erzurum_Gar%C4%B1). *(The WWI-origin claim is general knowledge not confirmed on that page — verify before publishing.)*

### Context card — a station opened three months earlier
Erzurum's railway station opened for service on Oct. 20, 1939, when the standard-gauge Sivas–Erzurum line reached the city ([Erzurum Garı (tr)](https://tr.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erzurum_Gar%C4%B1)). Fritz's party was among the first winter travellers on a line that snow and landslides were still closing for days at a time (Letter p. 12–13). In the meantime the line was also the earthquake zone's lifeline.

### Context card — dental tools as cookie molds
Fritz and Strauss carried dental instruments and tried to sell them in Erzurum to raise cash; impression trays (the "spoons" of `additional_info.md`) were offered to a baker as cookie molds (Letter p. 12). It shows how refugees' professional equipment became barter goods. (No free image of 1930s impression trays found; a modern CC photo of dental impression trays could illustrate.)

### Context card — the Erzincan earthquake
At 01:57 on Dec. 27, 1939 a magnitude 7.8 earthquake ruptured ~360 km of the North Anatolian Fault, destroying Erzincan; 32,968 people died and ~100,000 were injured, in extreme winter cold. It was Turkey's deadliest 20th-century earthquake and began a westward sequence of large North Anatolian Fault quakes. Fritz passed through 16 days later, saw survivors in unheated tents (Letter p. 14), and in Sivas met a 14-year-old boy who had lost his parents and three brothers. Source: [1939 Erzincan earthquake](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1939_Erzincan_earthquake).

### Context card — the new capital's station and beer
Ankara's current station building, designed by Şekip Akalın, opened in 1937, an Art Deco showpiece of the young Republic's capital ([Ankara railway station](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ankara_railway_station)). Ankara beer was brewed at the Atatürk Forest Farm brewery founded in 1933 (per the DGPI photo description on Commons; see also [Atatürk Forest Farm and Zoo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atat%C3%BCrk_Forest_Farm_and_Zoo)). After eleven days of tea, bread and mutton, "white-covered tables" and beer (Letter p. 15) meant they were back in the modern world.

### Context card
Haydarpaşa's terminal, designed by Otto Ritter and Helmuth Cuno and completed in 1909, was the Istanbul terminus of the Anatolian and Baghdad railways, with quays for direct ship–rail transfer ([Haydarpaşa Terminal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haydarpa%C5%9Fa_Terminal)). Because no bridge crossed the Bosphorus, every passenger from Asia continued by ferry (Letter p. 15).

## Stop: Istanbul — Turkish bath, the abscess, Goldenberg, the police court (Jan. 16–)

Letter p. 15–16: a cheap inn; two hours on the hot tiles of a Turkish bath; Mr. Goldenberg helps with money and a recommendation; the deep abscess is opened at the surgical department of the university clinic; then police and police-court trouble in Galata over registration, fines and threatened jail, foreign exchange and onward tickets.

### Context card — Istanbul University and émigré doctors
After the 1933 university reform, classes began on Nov. 1, 1933, and many German scientists — notably in medicine — who had been forced out of Nazi Germany, many of them Jewish, joined the faculty ([Istanbul University — history](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Istanbul_University)). It is plausible that the surgeons who opened Fritz's abscess (Letter p. 15) spoke German, but the letter doesn't say so — present as context only.

### Context card — the hamam
The Turkish bath (hamam) — hot marble platform, scrubbing and washing — was everyday public hygiene in 1940 Istanbul; after weeks without proper washing (Letter p. 6, 9) it was the travellers' first real wash. Source: [Hammam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hammam).
