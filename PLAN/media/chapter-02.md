## 1. Tabriz — organizing the onward journey (Dec 21)

Letter p. 2: Fritz changes his pinching shoes at the garage. Hamid-Aga takes them to "a pharmacy opposite" where an older gentleman translates via French. He turns out to be a Lithuanian Jew, **Isaac Rabinowitsch**, who "was helpful to us in a touching way." He takes them to a transport specialist, and his clerk takes them into the bazaar, where they book the trip to Trebizond with a firm's manager for **130 toman**: "a 1.5-ton Chevrolet with space for 5 persons." Then roast chicken at the hotel. In the bazaar, Mr. Fischer's acquaintance ("crossed legs, a black-framed moon face") sends for his English-speaking son, who gets them bus seats to Bazirgan for 1 toman.

**Context card: Chevrolets on the Persian roads.** In the 1930s, American-built trucks, especially Chevrolet and Ford 1½-tonners, carried most of Iran's long-distance road freight. Their simple six- and eight-cylinder engines could be repaired in roadside garages. Fritz's 1.5-ton Chevrolet had only three seats in the cab: when all five set off, "the chauffeur had to sit on the mudguard" (Letter p. 3). That truck and its crew (driver Ismael and manager Abdil) carry the story into Chapter 3. *(General context; I found no single citable source on Iranian truck fleets in 1939. Ask the parent whether to drop this card or source it.)*

**Context card: A Jewish pharmacist in Tabriz.** Tabriz had a small Jewish community in the 1930s, and a few Ashkenazi Jews from the former Russian Empire had settled in Persian Azerbaijan. Isaac Rabinowitsch, a Lithuanian Jew, recognized two fellow Jewish emigrants and helped them (Letter p. 2). *(No public-record match found for "Isaac Rabinowitsch, Tabriz." Family or JDC archive research could add to this; see the JDC Archives at https://archives.jdc.org/.)*

## 2. Transit: Tabriz → Khoy by crowded bus (evening of Dec 21 → 1 AM Dec 22, ~170 km)

Letter p. 2–3: the bus is "stuffed full. People were even sitting packed tightly in the aisle." They drive "initially along the railway line to Julfa, which we crossed many times." At an evening stop in a small courtyard they "drank tea in rough crowds and watched the men praying." They eat "a few hand towels of firmer quality and sheep's cheese," and pay 2 toman 10 shahi for 9 glasses of tea plus bread and cheese.

**Context card: The Tabriz–Julfa railway.** Imperial Russia built the 146 km line from Julfa on the Aras river, the Russian border, to Tabriz in 1912–1916; the first train reached Tabriz in March 1916. In 1939 it was still Tabriz's only railway, and it led to the Soviet Union, not toward Turkey ([Wikipedia: Tabriz railway station](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tabriz_railway_station); [Wikipedia: Azerbaijan (Iran)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Azerbaijan_(Iran))). The road to Khoy followed the line north through Sufian and Marand before branching west, so the bus "crossed [it] many times" (Letter p. 2). *(The route through Sufian and Marand is my inference from geography.)*

**Context card: "Hand towels" (lavash).** Fritz's running joke is the thin flatbread: "hand towels of firmer quality" (Letter p. 2), "hand-towels of lesser quality" (Letter p. 3), and "a towel" at lunch in Bazirgan (Letter p. 4). Lavash is rolled out paper-thin and slapped onto the wall of a clay oven (*tanur*), and it folds like cloth. Diners tear off a piece, wrap cheese or meat in it and roll it up, just as Fritz describes: "rolled it up like a giant cigarette" (Letter p. 3). UNESCO inscribed "flatbread making and sharing culture: Lavash, Katyrma, Jupka, Yufka," a joint nomination including Iran, Azerbaijan, Turkey, Kazakhstan and Kyrgyzstan, in 2016: https://ich.unesco.org/en/RL/flatbread-making-and-sharing-culture-lavash-katyrma-jupka-yufka-01181.

## 3. Khoy (1 AM Dec 22 → 3 PM)

Letter p. 3: their suitcases and the Chevrolet both arrive "and both made a huge noise." The hotel is closed, so a police officer helps them wake the receptionist. They get "the two-bed deluxe room" but sleep in one bed. Next morning in the bazaar they buy "thicker, colorful wool socks for 3 Toman each, which actually look more like slippers and are worn by almost all people there." Lunch is "excellent sour milk (*dough*)" and kebab.

**Context card: Knitted socks (*jurab*).** Patterned, hand-knitted wool socks (*jurab* in Persian, *corab* in Azerbaijani Turkish and Turkish) are a winter staple across Iranian Azerbaijan, eastern Anatolia and the Caucasus. They are thick enough to wear like slippers inside the house. Fritz paid 3 toman a pair (Letter p. 3). On the frozen night drive to Karaköse in Chapter 3, he wore "the mountain boots over thick colored socks on Strauss's advice" (Letter p. 5). *(Cultural description from general knowledge; I found no citable free source.)*

**Context card: *Dugh*.** *Dugh* (doogh) is yogurt thinned with water, salted, and often flavored with dried mint. It is drunk with kebab across Iran ([Wikipedia: Doogh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doogh)). Fritz glosses it in the letter as "sour milk" (Letter p. 3).

## 4. Transit: Khoy → Maku — the ancient bus (3 PM → 11 PM, Dec 22, ~130 km)

Letter p. 3: "an ancient bus, which was mainly loaded with high piles of junk." There were 7 passengers. "Slower than a pedestrian, the bus chugged up the first slope, and every 2 km it was stopped to refill large amounts of carried water into the boiling radiator." Then the bus drove along a broader river. At dusk they drank tea "in a picturesque opium den" and dug some Iranian pistachios out of the floor of their bag. About 10 km before Maku they met the Chevrolet again and ate dinner in a "dive bar": "hand-towels of lesser quality, an excellent dish and a porridge made of flour, sugar, and mutton fat." Letter p. 3 (Karagossa section): "From Tabriz to Maku, one could only describe the paths in short sections as roads… the ride through the high mountain landscape was overall delightful… in the sharp moonlight."

- **Buses:** I found no free 1930s photo of a Persian country bus. Dick Gilbert's 1970 overland-bus photos on Commons (CC BY 2.0; see 5.3) are the nearest free option. Wording for a vehicle card: "a 1920s–30s truck chassis with a wooden body, radiator boiling on long climbs."
- **Opium:** no free period photo found. The Leiden University Library A. P. H. Hotz collection (late-Qajar photos, public domain on Commons) is the best place to look further.

**Context card: Opium in 1930s Iran.** Opium smoking was common in Iran in the 1930s. The state ran an opium monopoly from 1928, taxing production and sales, and teahouses with opium pipes were ordinary roadside stops; production was banned only in 1955. *(Sources to cite: [Encyclopaedia Iranica, "Opium"](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/opium); I could not load it from my machine, so check the dates against it.)* Fritz mentions it in passing (Letter p. 3). Keep the tone light, as he does.

**Context card: The porridge of flour, sugar and mutton fat.** This sounds like *kachi* (or a thin *halva*): flour toasted in fat and sweetened with sugar or syrup. It is cheap, very high in calories, and a winter dish in northwestern Iran. *(This identification is my inference; present it as "probably.")*

**Context card: Boiling radiators.** On long climbs in the 1930s, engines boiled over easily. Drivers carried water cans and stopped to top up the radiator, here "every 2 km" (Letter p. 3). A 130 km leg took 8 hours. This is a good place to highlight how much of the travel was the road itself.

## 5. Maku (11 PM Dec 22 → 9 AM Dec 23)

Letter p. 3: "In Maku there was no hotel, not even a teahouse." They sleep on two benches with a mattress in the bus waiting room. Next morning: "the village, which sits in a narrow valley under a mighty, vertical rock wall… houses up to 100m height like birds' nests, and then came the wall, which threatened to crush everything below it." There was no water for washing.

**Context card: Maku.** Maku sits in a narrow gorge of the Zangmar river at about 1,634 m, 22 km from the Turkish border. ## 6. Transit: Maku → Bazirgan (9 AM Dec 23, ~4 km per the letter)

Letter p. 3: "now for the first time in the Chevrolet. As there were only 3 seats, the chauffeur had to sit on the mudguard and the manager drove a short distance together. Luckily it was only 4 km to Bazirgan."

**Distance flag:** Maku to the Bazargan border post is about 20 km by road today, not 4 km. Wikipedia gives Maku as 22 km from the border. Fritz may have meant 4 km from their last stop, or the old road may have run differently. `route.md`'s Maku coordinates (39.29 N, 44.45 E) and Bazargan coordinates (39.39 N, 44.39 E) are about 12 km apart in a straight line, so the coordinates are plausible but "4 km" is not. ## 7. Bazirgan (Bazargan) — Iranian customs (morning–midday, Dec 23)

Letter p. 3–4:
- **Setting:** "two massive buildings at the outermost northwest tip of Iran, in which the police and customs are housed," with sheds and the village tucked into the mountains.
- **Customs director:** a "stocky, graying gentleman in uniform" who speaks good French and "had 2 bad teeth that plagued him terribly." Fritz examines them "with important expressions" without instruments.
- **The search:** Strauss's photo album "seemed suspicious," but it ends with "These are only your personal effects in there!?"
- **Currency:** the director sells Fritz "an Iraqi [1?] note" (German transcription; denomination unclear) for 10 toman, and his assistant sells 7 Turkish lira for 14 toman.
- **Mount Ararat** is wrapped in "black, impenetrable fog," so no glimpse of "the ruins of Noah's Ark."
- **The Turkish airplane:** they visit "the ruins of the already mentioned Turkish airplane that wait there for pickup by the Turks."
- **Telegram** home from the village.
- **Lunch** in Abdil's two-room mud hut, furnished with "a samovar, a *silou* [carpet/mat], and a few cushions": chicken, fried potatoes, "a towel" and grapes.

**Context card: The Iranian customs post at Bazirgan.** Bazargan is Iran's main road crossing into Turkey, at the foot of the Ararat massif. The modern customs complex looks over the border gate (7.4). In 1939 it was, as Fritz says, "two massive buildings" plus goods sheds and a mud-brick village. The Turkish–Iranian border here was redrawn by a 1932 agreement in which Iran gave up the slopes of Little Ararat to Turkey in exchange for land further south. *(Verify the 1932 exchange against [Wikipedia: Iran–Turkey border](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iran%E2%80%93Turkey_border) before use; my machine was rate-limited and I couldn't open it.)*

**Context card: A dentist at the border?** Fritz "consulted the director about his teeth" and gave advice "as best we could in the absence of instruments and medication" (Letter p. 4). In Chapter 4 a local buys his "impression spoons" (see `additional_info.md` #2). Together these suggest Fritz was a dentist or dental technician. *(Confirm with the family; this is the kind of personal detail that makes the site.)*

**Context card: Ararat and Noah's Ark.** Greater Ararat (5,137 m) is the mountain traditionally linked with Noah's Ark (Genesis 8:4, "the mountains of Ararat"). Fritz joked that they "tried in vain to penetrate with our gazes to the ruins of Noah's Ark" (Letter p. 4). He saw the mountain properly the next night, "in moonlight," from the snowbound Ford truck (Letter p. 5; Chapter 3).

**Context card: The *silou*.** The translator glosses *silou* as "[carpet/mat]." It is probably the Persian ***zilu***, a flat-woven cotton floor rug made in central Iran (Meybod, Yazd) and long used in homes, mosques and teahouses. *(My suggestion; check against the German typescript spelling.)*

**Denomination caveat (Iraqi note):** Fritz paid 10 toman (100 rials) for "an Iraqi 5-note." In 1939 the Iraqi dinar was pegged to the pound sterling, so a 5-dinar note would have been worth far more than 100 rials. The note was probably a smaller denomination (5 fils? ¼ dinar?), or the "5" refers to something else. Caption 7.5 and 7.6 as "an Iraqi banknote of the 1930s," not "the 5-dinar note Fritz bought."

**Context card: Why the tires were taken.** Customs officers confiscated "2 new tires" from the driver (Letter p. 4). ## Flags: possible errors in the plan docs (Chapter 2)

1. **Telegram date (resolved).** The German dateline is "25. Dezember 1939" (the English had "29"), so they left Tehran on Dec 19 and reached Bazirgan on Dec 23, which matches the telegram. Original flag: Letter p. 1 says the telegram was sent "from Bazirgan on the 23rd," but by the letter's own timeline they reached Bazirgan on **Dec 27** (Letter p. 3–4), and Dec 23 was the departure from Tehran. This is probably a transcription or translation slip; check the German typescript.
2. **`pages/12.md` (Tabriz → Khoy)** describes the boiling radiator, the opium den, meeting the Chevrolet 10 km before Maku, and the flatbread dinner. All of these happen on the **Khoy → Maku** leg (Letter p. 3). The Tabriz → Khoy leg was the overcrowded evening bus along the Julfa railway, with a courtyard tea stop, men praying, and "hand towels" with sheep's cheese (Letter p. 2). The plan also dates this page "Dec 25 (Evening)," which is right for Tabriz → Khoy.
3. **`pages/13.md` (Khoy)** says only "overnight rest." It misses the night police episode, the wool socks bought in the bazaar, and *dugh* with kebab (Letter p. 3). "After driving through dark mountain roads" is not in the letter for this leg.
4. **`pages/11.md` (Tabriz)** says the pharmacist helped secure "bus tickets to Bazargan." The pharmacist (Isaac Rabinowitsch) arranged the **truck** (130 toman to Trebizond). The **bus** seat (1 toman) was arranged by Mr. Fischer's acquaintance's English-speaking son (Letter p. 2). Hamid-Aga's Thursday truck to Trebizond was an offer, not the booking they made.
5. **`design_doc.md`, Ch. 2, "toothless border director."** He had **two bad teeth**, not none (Letter p. 4).
6. **`design_doc.md`, Ch. 2, "Mountain Village Socks… hand-knitted raw wool."** The letter says "thicker, colorful wool socks… look more like slippers." "Raw wool" is not in the letter; "colorful" is.
7. **`design_doc.md` heading "The Anatolian Frontier"** vs the parent's "The Persian Frontier." Persian is more accurate, since everything except the final Gürbulak stop is in Iran.
8. **`design_doc.md`, Ch. 1 "Sangak & Lavash" card** belongs in Chapter 2, and should say lavash (see Chapter 1 flag 2).
9. **`additional_info.md` #8** says the breads were "often wrapped in actual cloth towels." That may be true, but Fritz's joke is about the bread's shape and texture. The letter does not mention cloth.
