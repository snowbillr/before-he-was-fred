# Chapter 1 — Departure from Tehran: media & context research

**Route:** Tehran → Qazvin → Zanjan → Taserkand → Mianeh → Tabriz (Dec 23–24, 1939)
**Letter pages:** p. 1–2 (`### Page 1`, `### Page 2` in `PLAN/translation.md`)

## Notes on sources and licenses

- **Annemarie Schwarzenbach (1908–1942)** was a Swiss writer and photographer who drove the Tehran–Tabriz road several times in 1933–1935 and was in Iran again in 1939–40. Her negatives are held by the Swiss Literary Archives / Swiss National Library (CH-NB) and are mirrored on Wikimedia Commons as **public domain**, since she died in 1942 (more than 70 years ago). They are the best period-matched images for this chapter. Each file page shows "Public domain" plus CH-NB provenance. Credit line: *Annemarie Schwarzenbach / Swiss National Library, Swiss Literary Archives (public domain)*.
- **Antoin Sevruguin (c. 1851–1933)** was a Tehran court photographer. His work is public domain; the Commons copies come from Museum Rietberg Zürich.
- **Eugène Flandin (1840 engravings)** are public domain but much older than 1939. Use them only for places that changed little, such as bridges and cliffs.
- I could not reach the Library of Congress from my research machine (its bot check returned HTTP 403), so it is not used here. Searching loc.gov for "Tabriz" or "Persia" by hand may turn up a few more items.
- Licenses below were checked against the Commons file metadata (`LicenseShortName`). CC BY / CC BY-SA items need attribution, and CC BY-SA items carry share-alike terms.

**Context card: Why a hired car?** The Trans-Iranian Railway, opened in 1938, ran north–south from the Caspian Sea to the Persian Gulf. The line from Tehran to Tabriz was not finished until 1958 ([Wikipedia: Tabriz railway station](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tabriz_railway_station)). In December 1939 the only way to travel overland from Tehran toward Turkey was by road: hired cars, buses and trucks on mostly unpaved highways (Letter p. 1, p. 3).

**Context card: Refugees leaving Iran, 1939.** Fritz and Strauss describe themselves as "Jewish emigrants" (Letter p. 2) and are grateful for "being able to escape the immediate danger zone" (Letter p. 1). War had broken out in Europe in September 1939, so the usual sea routes were closed or dangerous. The overland route through Turkey to an Italian port was one of the few ways left to reach America. *(Background to be filled in from family records: when and how Fritz got to Iran. The letter itself only says "the last 2 years.")*

**Context card: The Grand Hotel of Qazvin.** According to Wikipedia, the hotel was built in 1922 in the western wing of the Chehel Sotun palace complex, under governor Sa'd as-Saltaneh, by the Qazvin architect Ostad Ali July. Visitors described its furniture and decoration as European in style. It is linked to the planning of Reza Khan's 1921 coup. *(Note the date clash between "1922" and the 1921 coup; the article is thinly sourced.)* Qazvin was the junction where the Tehran road split toward Rasht on the Caspian and toward Tabriz, so its hotel served people traveling between Europe and Tehran. Strauss had stayed there for 4 days on his way into Iran (Letter p. 1). Source: [Wikipedia: Grand Hotel, Qazvin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grand_Hotel,_Qazvin).

**Context card: Toman, rial and shahi.** Iran replaced the *qiran* with the *rial* in 1932, but people kept counting money in **toman** (1 toman = 10 rials). Small change was counted in **shahi** (20 shahi = 1 rial). Fritz's "2 Toman and 10 Chahi" (Letter p. 3) means 2 toman and 10 shahi. Prices in the letter for comparison: a snack in Qazvin cost 8 toman, a bus seat to Bazirgan 1 toman, the truck to Trebizond 130 toman, and wool socks 3 toman (Letter p. 1–3). Source: [Wikipedia: Iranian rial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iranian_rial). Image: 500-rial note, Bank Melli Iran, 1938 — https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/8/89/500_Rials_banknote_Reza_Shah.JPG ([page](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:500_Rials_banknote_Reza_Shah.JPG), public domain).

Letter p. 1–2: only one hotel; rooms locked and keys lost; the two men share a single bed for warmth; the young man complains his window "had no panes." In the morning Fritz notices that wooden shutters, not glass, were the local custom.

**Context card: Shutters instead of glass.** Fritz's joke about the window "without panes" (Letter p. 2) reflects building practice on the northwestern plateau. Glass was expensive and had to be carried in by road, so many rural inns and houses closed their window openings with solid wooden shutters. *(No specific source found; present it as Fritz's observation, not as a sourced fact.)*

**Context card: Qaflankuh and the "Maiden's Bridge."** The Qaflankuh mountains, up to about 2,970 m, run north-west to south-east across the old Tehran–Tabriz road. The Qezel Owzan river cuts through them just before Mianeh ([Wikipedia: Qaflankuh Mountain Range](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qaflankuh_Mountain_Range)). The many-arched brick bridge there, known locally as Pol-e Dokhtar / Qız Körpüsü, was drawn by Flandin in 1840. In winter the zigzag climb over Qaflankuh was notorious for snowdrifts. Fritz noted "many other cars that were stuck in the snow" while their driver got through (Letter p. 2).

**Context card: The royal wedding of 1939.** Crown Prince Mohammad Reza Pahlavi married Princess Fawzia of Egypt, sister of King Farouk, at Cairo's Abdeen Palace in March 1939. A second round of celebrations followed in Tehran in April 1939, with foreign delegations attending. **Unverified:** the crash of "2 Turkish airplanes" on the way to Tabriz (Letter p. 2), and the wreck "waiting for pickup" at Bazirgan (Letter p. 4). I found no Wikipedia or Commons record of a Turkish military air accident in Iran in 1939. Before presenting it as fact, check Turkish Air Force history sources or 1939 newspapers (*Cumhuriyet*, *Ettela'at*, or the *New York Times* scans on Internet Archive). Until then, frame it as "the driver told them…".

**Context card: Turkmenchay and the road to Tabriz.** *(Inference.)* A point about 100 km before Tabriz on the old road is near **Turkmenchay**, the village where the 1828 treaty was signed that ceded the Caucasus khanates to Russia ([Wikipedia: Treaty of Turkmenchay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treaty_of_Turkmenchay)). The "endless serpentines over a high mountain" after it fit the Shibli pass climb east of Tabriz. Both identifications are my reading of the distances, not something the letter states.

**Context card: Tabriz in 1939.** Tabriz was Iran's second-largest city and its gateway to Turkey and the Caucasus. The only railway out of it ran north to the Soviet border at Julfa (built by Russia in 1912–1916). Around 1930 Reza Shah's modernization had cut wide new avenues through the old quarters (11.7). The skyline was dominated by the massive brick **Arg** (14th-century Ilkhanid citadel and mosque of Alishah) and the shell of the **Blue Mosque** (finished 1465, ruined by the 1780 earthquake). Sources: [Arg of Tabriz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arg_of_Tabriz), [Blue Mosque, Tabriz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blue_Mosque,_Tabriz).

## Flags: possible errors in the plan docs (Chapter 1)

1. **`design_doc.md`, Ch. 1, "Late-night departure."** The letter says they left at 5 PM "in the twilight" (Letter p. 1).
2. **`design_doc.md`, Ch. 1, flatbread "hand-towels" and Sangak.** No flatbread is eaten in Chapter 1. The "hand towels" first appear at the evening bus stop between Tabriz and Khoy (Letter p. 2, end) and at dinner near Maku (Letter p. 3), so the card belongs in **Chapter 2**. Also, the description (tear "a rag off the hand-towel," roll it "like a giant cigarette") fits thin **lavash**, the everyday bread of Persian Azerbaijan, much better than thick, pebble-baked **sangak**. `additional_info.md` #8 hedges with "Lavash or Sangak"; I recommend saying lavash.
3. **`pages/03.md`: "Their wealthy companion Landrock charged them 8 Toman."** The sentence is ambiguous: "Our bread was meant to spare that of the affluent Landrock, which we had taken along; he took 8 Toman from us." Landrock seems to be the person who supplied their travel bread, probably a Tehran acquaintance; he was not one of the four in the car (Fritz, Strauss, the girl and the young man). "He" who charged 8 toman is most likely the hotel. Check the German.
4. **`pages/10.md`: the luggage bus was "delayed for four days."** The letter says it "had already been on the road for 4 days." It was slow, not necessarily stranded.
5. **`route.md`: Taserkand coordinates** probably too close to Mianeh (see §7).
6. **`additional_info.md` intro** lists Tehran, Tabriz, Erzurum and others as "major world cities." That's fine, but Mianeh, Zanjan and Qazvin were also real towns in 1939, not hamlets. No change needed.
7. **Unverified event:** the Turkish airplane crash after the Crown Prince's wedding (see §10).
