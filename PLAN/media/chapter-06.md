### Context card

**Card — A terminal built for liners (Letter p. 20)**
The Fascist government wanted Naples to be a leading Mediterranean port, and in 1933 it put a new maritime station out to tender. The architect Cesare Bazzani designed it, and the Ferrobeton company built it on a widened quay between 1934 and 1936. To make room, the old Molo San Gennaro and the city's famous harbour *lanterna* were destroyed. The building has two wings of about 182 m each, and a passage under the central block was designed so that boat trains could pull up right beside the ships. On westbound voyages the Italian Line's express liners sailed from Genoa and called at Naples before heading for Gibraltar and New York, which is why Fritz could board at Naples. *Sources: [Stazione marittima di Napoli — Wikipedia (it)](https://it.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stazione_marittima_di_Napoli); [italianliners.com](https://www.italianliners.com/conte-di-savoia-en).*

### Context cards

**Card — "The ship that doesn't roll" (Letter p. 20)**
The *Conte di Savoia* was ordered by Lloyd Sabaudo, launched at Trieste on 28 October 1931 and completed in 1932 for the new state-backed *Italia Flotte Riunite* (from 1937 *Italia Società di Navigazione*). At 48,502 GRT she was the running mate of the slightly larger *Rex* on the Genoa–Naples–Gibraltar–New York express route. She was the first major liner fitted with gyroscopic anti-roll stabilizers: three huge Sperry gyroscopes in a forward hold. In practice they made her "hang" at the end of a roll, so they were used mainly on westbound crossings like Fritz's. Her interiors, by the Trieste designer Gustavo Pulitzer Finali, were strikingly modern and very popular with American passengers. *Sources: [SS Conte di Savoia — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SS_Conte_di_Savoia); [Conte di Savoia — Wikipedia (it)](https://it.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conte_di_Savoia_(transatlantico)); [italianliners.com](https://www.italianliners.com/conte-di-savoia-en).*

**Card — A neutral liner full of refugees (Letter p. 20)**
When war broke out in September 1939, Italy stayed out of it. The Italian Line kept the *Rex* and *Conte di Savoia* running to New York, and from 15 September 1939 the *Conte di Savoia* sailed with neutrality markings painted on her sides. For German and Austrian Jews who had visas for the United States, the Italian liners from Genoa and Naples were among the last regular ways across the Atlantic. Fritz notes that "by far the majority of the passengers were emigrants coming out of Germany." The service did not last long. The *Conte di Savoia* left New York on her last crossing on 25 May 1940 and was laid up near Venice after Italy entered the war on 10 June 1940. In 1943 she was set on fire by German aircraft and sank. *Sources: [italianliners.com chronology](https://www.italianliners.com/conte-di-savoia-en); [SS Conte di Savoia — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SS_Conte_di_Savoia).* (For a sourced statement about how many refugees travelled on Italian liners in 1939–40, try the USHMM Holocaust Encyclopedia before publishing. I did not verify a specific figure.)

### Context card

**Card — Skat, "the king of German card games" (Letter p. 20)**
Skat is a three-player trick-taking game with a 32-card pack. Members of a card club in Altenburg, Thuringia, devised it around 1810–1813, and it became Germany's national card game. Players bid for the right to choose trumps, and the winner of the bid plays alone against the other two. Earlier in the journey Fritz and Strauss passed the time with the two-player game *Sixty-six* (see `additional_info.md` #4). On the ship they finally had enough players for Skat. *Source: [Skat (card game) — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Skat_(card_game)).*

### Context card

**Card — Contraband control at Gibraltar (Letter p. 20)**
As soon as war began in September 1939, Britain revived its First World War blockade of Germany. Neutral merchant ships and liners passing British-controlled choke points, Gibraltar among them, were stopped so that boarding officers could check cargo, mail and passengers for anything bound for the enemy, including German men of military age. Italian liners were "often stopped for many hours at Gibraltar by units of the Royal Navy and subjected to an inspection of passengers and cargo," according to the Italian Liners Historical Society. Fritz's six hours at anchor are typical. In late May 1940, shortly before Italy entered the war, the *Conte di Savoia*'s captain raced through the Strait at night, close to the African shore, to avoid being searched. *Sources: [italianliners.com](https://www.italianliners.com/conte-di-savoia-en); [Blockade of Germany (1939–1945) — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blockade_of_Germany_(1939%E2%80%931945)).*

### Context cards

**Card — Arriving at the West Side "Luxury Liner Row" (Letter p. 20)**
Big transatlantic liners did not dock in Brooklyn. They tied up at the new municipal piers on the Hudson River between West 44th and 54th Streets, which were built in the 1920s–30s because ships had outgrown the old Chelsea Piers. Piers 88–92, each 1,100 ft long, were finished between 1935 and 1937 for the largest liners. The Italian Line's chronology says the *Conte di Savoia* moved to the new super-pier 90 when it was finished. A ship coming in from the Narrows passed the Statue of Liberty and the towers of Lower Manhattan, then sailed up the Hudson past the whole West Side skyline. That matches Fritz's description of skyscrapers "appearing as the first ones faded away". *Sources: [Manhattan Cruise Terminal — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manhattan_Cruise_Terminal); [italianliners.com](https://www.italianliners.com/conte-di-savoia-en).*

**Card / feature — Passenger Ledger (finding the manifest)**
The passenger list for this arrival will be in U.S. National Archives microfilm series **T715, *Passenger and Crew Lists of Vessels Arriving at New York, NY, 1897–1957***. It is indexed for free (with registration) on [FamilySearch](https://www.familysearch.org/) (collection "New York Passenger Arrival Lists") and in the [Statue of Liberty–Ellis Island Foundation passenger search](https://heritage.statueofliberty.org/), which covers arrivals through 1957. Search for *Riesenfeld, Fritz* with the ship name *Conte di Savoia* and arrival around early February 1940. The manifest gives the exact arrival date and records age, nationality, "race or people" (often entered as "Hebrew" for Jewish immigrants in this period), last residence, nearest relative abroad, US contact and physical description. It would be the ideal primary source for the *Passenger Ledger* widget. **I did not run this search**, since both sites need a login. Manifest images are US federal records and generally public domain, but FamilySearch's site terms limit bulk reuse, so download the single image and credit NARA.

## Flags — possible errors in plan docs

1. **design_doc.md Ch. 6: "Arrival in NYC in late February 1940"** is wrong. Feb 21, 1940 is the date the letter was *written*. The ship left Naples on Jan 24 and Gibraltar on Jan 26 (Letter p. 20). Gibraltar–New York is about 3,200 nautical miles, roughly 5 days at the ship's 27-knot service speed, and the letter mentions at least three days at sea after Gibraltar. That puts the arrival at about **Jan 31 – Feb 2, 1940**. Confirm with the T715 manifest. `route.md` ("Early February 1940") is right. The design-doc route header "Jan 24 – Feb 21, 1940" mixes the arrival with the letter date.
2. **pages/27.md: "glided past the Statue of Liberty"** is not in the letter. Fritz mentions only the Manhattan skyscrapers rising out of the haze. The statue is geographically certain on that approach, but present it as context, not quotation.
3. **pages/27.md / design_doc.md: "severe Atlantic seasickness"** overstates the letter, which says "right on the verge of seasickness".
4. **"British contraband inspection"** (design_doc.md, pages/27.md) is a fair inference, well supported by italianliners.com, but the letter only says "the British authorities came aboard and left again". Phrase the card accordingly.
